Data Scientist interviews blend **statistics**, **modeling**, and
**experimentation** with coding and communication. Expect probability
and stats questions, a modeling case ("how would you predict X?"), an
A/B-test design discussion, and usually a Python/pandas or SQL screen.

## What interviewers probe

- **Statistics & probability**, distributions, hypothesis testing, p-values, confidence intervals, Bayes.
- **Modeling**, bias–variance trade-off, regularization, cross-validation, feature engineering, metric choice.
- **Experimentation**, A/B test design, power, sample size, novelty effects, pitfalls.
- **Coding**, pandas/NumPy data wrangling and SQL.
- **Communication**, explaining a model and its caveats to a non-technical stakeholder.

## Sample concept check

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`A model scores 99% accuracy on the training set but 72% on held-out data. What is the most likely diagnosis?

- Nothing is wrong; 99% training accuracy means the model is excellent.
  > Training accuracy alone is misleading; generalization to unseen data is what matters, and the gap here signals a problem.
- The test set is simply easier than the training set.
  > That would make test accuracy *higher*, not lower, the opposite of what's observed.
- Underfitting (high bias).
  > Underfitting shows up as *poor* performance on the training set too, here training accuracy is very high, so bias isn't the problem.
- [o] Overfitting (high variance), the model memorized the training data and generalizes poorly.
  > A large train-vs-test gap is the signature of overfitting. Remedies: more data, regularization, a simpler model, or better cross-validation.`}
/>
