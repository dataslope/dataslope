## Q&A

### Explain the bias–variance trade-off.

**Bias** is error from over-simplifying (underfitting); **variance** is
error from being too sensitive to the training data (overfitting). Simple
models → high bias, low variance; flexible models → low bias, high
variance. The goal is the sweet spot that minimizes total error on unseen
data — tuned via model complexity, regularization, and more data.

### How do you detect and fix overfitting?

Detect it as a large gap between **training** and **validation** scores
(great on train, poor on held-out). Fixes: more data, a simpler model,
**regularization** (L1/L2, dropout), early stopping, and proper
**cross-validation** so the estimate is honest.

### L1 vs L2 regularization?

Both penalize large coefficients to curb overfitting. **L1 (lasso)** can
drive coefficients to **exactly zero**, performing feature selection
(sparse models). **L2 (ridge)** shrinks them toward zero but rarely to
zero. Elastic net combines both.

### What is cross-validation and why use it?

Instead of one train/test split, **k-fold CV** rotates which fold is the
validation set and averages the score — a less noisy, less luck-dependent
estimate of generalization that also uses all data for both training and
validation across folds. For time series, use a **forward-chaining**
split (never validate on the past).

### Precision vs recall — when do you favor each?

**Precision** = of predicted positives, how many were right; **recall** =
of actual positives, how many you caught. Favor **recall** when misses
are costly (disease screening, fraud); favor **precision** when false
alarms are costly (spam filters flagging real mail). **F1** balances the
two.

- The model is excellent — 99% accuracy is hard to beat.

- [o] Accuracy misleads on imbalanced data; precision/recall (or F1 / PR-AUC) better reflect performance on the rare class.
