## Statistics

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the difference between a parameter and a statistic?

- A parameter is numeric; a statistic can be categorical.
  > Both are numeric summaries.
- [o] A parameter describes a population, a statistic a sample.
  > The parameter is usually unknown and the statistic estimates it. The Greek-versus-Latin notation convention (μ and σ against x̄ and s) exists to keep the two visually distinct.
- A parameter is estimated and a statistic is known exactly.
  > It is the other way round.
- They are the same quantity at different sample sizes.
  > Sample size never turns a statistic into a parameter.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does the standard error differ from the standard deviation?

- The standard deviation shrinks steadily as the sample size grows.
  > It converges to the population value; it does not shrink toward zero.
- [o] One describes observations, the other describes an estimate.
  > The standard deviation is the spread of individual values; the standard error is the spread of a *sample statistic* across repeated samples, and it shrinks as n grows. It is why a confidence interval narrows with more data while the underlying distribution's spread stays put.
- The standard error applies only to proportions.
  > It applies to any estimator.
- They are the same, computed differently.
  > They describe different things.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does the Central Limit Theorem state, and what does it not?

- That large samples are normally distributed.
  > The *data* need not be; the theorem is about the sampling distribution of the mean.
- [o] The sampling distribution of the mean approaches normality.
  > That holds as n grows, whatever the population's shape, and it says nothing about the shape of the data itself. It is why a t-test on skewed data is often still fine at a reasonable sample size.
- That the sample mean converges to the population mean.
  > That is the Law of Large Numbers.
- That any dataset becomes normal given a large enough number of observations.
  > Income data stays skewed no matter how much you collect.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does the Law of Large Numbers differ from the CLT?

- The LLN requires normality.
  > It requires a finite mean, not a distributional shape.
- [o] One promises accuracy, the other describes the uncertainty.
  > The LLN says the sample mean converges to the true mean; the CLT describes the *shape* of its distribution around that value. One promises accuracy, the other characterizes the remaining uncertainty.
- They are equivalent statements.
  > They make different claims.
- The LLN applies to proportions and the CLT applies to means.
  > Both apply to either.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`A test for a disease affecting 1 in 1,000 has 99% sensitivity and 99% specificity. A random person tests positive. Roughly what is the chance they have it?

- About 1%.
  > That is the prevalence, ignoring the test's evidence entirely.
- About 99%.
  > This confuses the test's accuracy with the probability of disease given a positive result.
- [o] About 9%.
  > Of 100,000 people, 100 have it and about 99 test positive; 99,900 do not and about 999 falsely test positive. So 99 / (99 + 999) ≈ 9%. Base rates dominate for rare conditions.
- About 50%.
  > That would need a far higher prevalence.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does a 95% confidence interval actually mean?

- The estimate is accurate to within 5%.
  > The width is set by variability and sample size, not by a percentage of the estimate.
- There is a 95% probability the true value lies in this interval.
  > Under the frequentist reading the true value is fixed; it is the interval that is random.
- 95% of the data lies within the interval.
  > That would be a prediction or tolerance interval.
- [o] About 95% of intervals built this way capture the truth.
  > That is a statement about the long-run behavior of the procedure, not about this one interval. The Bayesian credible interval is the one that supports the "95% probability" phrasing, and it requires a prior.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When do you use a t-test rather than a z-test?

- [o] When the population standard deviation has to be estimated.
  > Which is nearly always, in practice. The t-distribution's heavier tails account for that extra uncertainty, and converge to the normal as n grows.
- When the underlying data is not remotely normally distributed.
  > Non-normality calls for a non-parametric test or a transformation.
- When comparing more than two groups.
  > That is ANOVA.
- Whenever the sample is smaller than 100.
  > Sample size is a rough proxy, not the criterion.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a chi-square test of independence used for?

- Measuring the strength of a linear relationship.
  > That is correlation.
- [o] Testing whether two categorical variables are associated.
  > It compares observed cell counts against those expected under independence. The usual caution is small expected counts, where Fisher's exact test is more appropriate.
- Testing whether a sample is drawn from a normal distribution.
  > A goodness-of-fit variant can, but the independence test is the standard use.
- Comparing two means.
  > That is a t-test.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does ANOVA test?

- Whether a regression coefficient is zero.
  > That is a t-test on the coefficient.
- Whether two variances are equal.
  > That is an F-test of variances, related but different.
- Whether the model residuals follow a normal distribution.
  > That is a diagnostic, not what ANOVA tests.
- [o] Whether the means of three or more groups are all equal.
  > It works by comparing between-group with within-group variability. A significant result says at least one group differs, without saying which; that needs a post-hoc test with a multiplicity correction.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What exactly is a p-value?

- The probability the result will replicate.
  > Replication depends on the true effect and the next study's power.
- [o] The chance of data this extreme if the null were true.
  > It says nothing about effect size, and a very small p-value on a huge sample can accompany a trivially small effect.
- One minus the confidence level.
  > That is the significance threshold, α, which is chosen rather than computed.
- The probability that the null hypothesis is actually true.
  > It is computed *assuming* the null, so it cannot also measure its truth.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is statistical power, and what raises it?

- [o] The chance of detecting a real effect when one exists.
  > A larger sample, a larger true effect, lower variance, or a higher α all raise it. Power is the quantity that determines whether "no significant difference" is informative or meaningless.
- The width of the confidence interval.
  > Width reflects precision; power is a probability about the test's behavior.
- The probability of a Type I error.
  > That is α.
- The magnitude of whatever effect happened to be observed.
  > That is effect size.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the difference between a Type I and a Type II error?

- Type I is a measurement error; Type II is a sampling error.
  > Both are inferential errors, not data errors.
- Type II occurs only in one-sided tests.
  > It occurs in both.
- [o] One is a false positive, the other a false negative.
  > Type I rejects a true null at rate α; Type II fails to reject a false null at rate β, and power is 1 − β. Lowering α without raising the sample size trades false positives for false negatives.
- Type I is always more serious.
  > Which is worse depends entirely on the decision at stake.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why correct for multiple comparisons?

- [o] Twenty tests at α = 0.05 give a 64% chance of a false positive.
  > The family-wise error rate needs controlling once you are running many tests. Bonferroni is the blunt, conservative option; Benjamini-Hochberg controls the false discovery rate and retains far more power.
- Because p-values are biased upward.
  > Individual p-values remain valid; it is the selection among them that misleads.
- Because each test uses the same data.
  > Reuse is a related concern, but the mechanism is the number of chances.
- Because the correction makes the results look more significant.
  > Corrections make results *less* likely to be declared significant.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is p-hacking?

- Computing a p-value incorrectly.
  > A calculation error is a different mistake.
- [o] Trying variations until something crosses 0.05, then reporting it.
  > Extra covariates, different subgroups, and more data collection are all ways in. The published p-value is then uninterpretable, because the search that produced it is invisible. Pre-registration is the structural fix.
- Reporting a p-value without an accompanying confidence interval.
  > Incomplete reporting, but not p-hacking.
- Using a one-sided test.
  > A one-sided test chosen in advance for a directional hypothesis is legitimate.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What distinguishes a Bayesian from a frequentist approach?

- Bayesian methods are simply unable to produce point estimates.
  > Posterior mean, median, and mode are all point estimates.
- [o] Whether the parameter or the data is treated as random.
  > Bayesians put a prior on the parameter and update it into a posterior; frequentists hold the parameter fixed and treat the data as random. It is why only the Bayesian framework licenses "there is a 95% probability the effect is between x and y".
- Bayesian methods do not use probability.
  > They are built on it.
- Frequentist methods require larger samples.
  > Sample requirements depend on the question, not the philosophy.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When would you use a non-parametric test?

- Whenever the sample is small.
  > Small samples matter, but the assumption is what decides.
- [o] When the parametric assumptions are badly violated.
  > Or when the data is ordinal to begin with. Mann-Whitney for two groups, Kruskal-Wallis for several. The cost is less power when the parametric assumptions would have held.
- When you want a smaller p-value.
  > Choosing a test by the p-value it produces is p-hacking.
- When the dataset happens to contain some missing values.
  > Missingness is handled separately.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is Maximum Likelihood Estimation?

- Maximizing the correlation between predictions and outcomes.
  > That is a different objective.
- [o] Choosing the parameters that make the data most probable.
  > Under the assumed model, that is. For linear regression with normal errors, MLE and ordinary least squares give the same answer, which is a useful anchor.
- Minimizing the number of parameters.
  > That is a regularization or model-selection concern.
- Choosing the parameter with the highest prior probability.
  > That is a Bayesian MAP estimate with no data.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is Simpson's paradox?

- A correlation that disappears once more data arrives.
  > That is usually just noise resolving.
- An effect that is significant but small.
  > That is a significance-versus-practicality issue.
- [o] A trend that reverses once you split by subgroup.
  > It happens when an unbalanced confounder is mixed into the totals. The famous Berkeley admissions case is the standard example. Which view is correct depends on the causal structure, not on which one is larger.
- A correlation without causation.
  > Related, but the paradox is specifically a reversal under stratification.`}
/>

## Probability

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does \`P(A | B)\` mean?

- [o] The probability of A given that B has occurred, computed as \`P(A ∩ B) / P(B)\`.
  > Conditioning restricts the sample space to B, which is why the denominator is \`P(B)\` rather than 1.
- The probability of A and B both occurring at the same time.
  > That is the joint probability.
- The probability of A or B.
  > That is the union.
- The probability of B given A.
  > The order matters, and confusing the two is exactly the base-rate fallacy.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the difference between independent and mutually exclusive events?

- Independence requires equal probabilities.
  > Probabilities are unconstrained.
- [o] Independence tells you nothing; exclusivity tells you a lot.
  > If one of two mutually exclusive events occurs, the other definitely did not, which is maximal dependence rather than independence. Two mutually exclusive events with non-zero probability are therefore necessarily dependent.
- Mutually exclusive events are always independent of each other.
  > Knowing one occurred forces the other's probability to zero, which is maximal dependence.
- They are the same property.
  > They are nearly opposites for events with non-zero probability.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is linearity of expectation so useful?

- It lets you multiply expectations.
  > \`E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]\` requires independence.
- It applies only to discrete variables.
  > It holds for continuous variables too.
- [o] \`E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]\` holds even under dependence.
  > Problems with messy dependence often become trivial as a result. The classic use is counting expected matches by summing indicator variables that are heavily dependent on each other.
- It only ever holds for genuinely independent variables.
  > It holds regardless of dependence, which is precisely why it is useful.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Which distribution models the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials?

- [o] Binomial.
  > Fixed n, constant success probability p, independent trials. With one trial it reduces to Bernoulli.
- Poisson.
  > Poisson counts events over an interval with no fixed trial count.
- Geometric.
  > Geometric counts trials until the first success.
- Exponential.
  > Exponential models continuous waiting time.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does it mean that the exponential distribution is memoryless?

- Its expected value is zero.
  > Its mean is 1/λ.
- [o] The remaining wait does not depend on the wait so far.
  > It is why exponential inter-arrival times pair with a Poisson count, and why "the bus is overdue so it must be due soon" is wrong under this model.
- It has no variance.
  > It has a well-defined variance.
- Past events have no bearing on the distribution's parameters.
  > Parameters are fixed by definition; memorylessness is about the conditional distribution.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`In the Monty Hall problem, should you switch, and why?

- No; both remaining doors are equally likely.
  > This ignores that the host's choice is constrained by knowledge.
- Yes, because the probability becomes 1/2.
  > Switching wins 2/3 of the time, not 1/2.
- [o] Yes, because your original door keeps its 1/3 probability.
  > The host's informed reveal concentrates the remaining 2/3 on the other door. If the host opened a door at random and it happened to be empty, the intuition would change; the host's knowledge is the load-bearing assumption.
- Switching makes no difference at all to the long-run outcome.
  > Switching doubles the win rate.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`In a room of 23 people, roughly what is the chance two share a birthday?

- About 23%, matching the number of people.
  > This mistakes the count of people for a probability.
- About 6%.
  > That is closer to the chance someone shares *your* birthday.
- [o] About 50%.
  > There are 253 pairs, not 23 comparisons, which is why the answer feels far too high.
- About 99%.
  > That takes around 57 people.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the difference between covariance and correlation?

- They differ only in sign convention.
  > They differ in scale.
- Covariance is always positive.
  > It takes either sign.
- Correlation captures non-linear relationships and covariance does not.
  > Both are linear measures.
- [o] Correlation is covariance standardized to a unitless scale.
  > Covariance is in the product of the original units; correlation runs between −1 and 1. It is why covariance values cannot be compared across variable pairs and correlations can.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`A fair coin is flipped 10 times and lands heads every time. What is the probability the next flip is heads?

- Less than 1/2, since tails is due.
  > That is the gambler's fallacy.
- It cannot be determined.
  > The problem states the coin is fair.
- [o] 1/2, since the coin is stated to be fair.
  > Flips are independent. That said, 10 heads is itself weak evidence to re-examine the fairness assumption. Both halves matter: independence given fairness, and the sensible instinct to question fairness after an unlikely run.
- More than 1/2, since the coin is clearly biased.
  > Under the stated assumption of fairness it is exactly 1/2; suspecting bias is a separate, Bayesian, question.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the expected value of rolling a fair six-sided die?

- 3, the middle value.
  > The middle of 1 to 6 is 3.5, and no single face is the mean.
- 1, on the grounds that each face has probability 1/6.
  > That confuses a probability with an outcome.
- 6, the maximum.
  > That is the maximum, not the mean.
- [o] 3.5, the average of the six equally likely outcomes.
  > An expectation need not be an attainable value, which is often the point of the question.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`State Bayes' theorem and say when you would reach for it.

- [o] \`P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A) / P(B)\`, used to invert a conditional.
  > It turns "probability of the evidence given the hypothesis" into "probability of the hypothesis given the evidence". Diagnostic testing, spam filtering, and any question where the base rate matters more than the test's accuracy.
- \`P(A|B) = P(A)P(B)\`, which is used whenever two events are independent of one another.
  > That is the definition of independence, not Bayes' theorem.
- \`P(A|B) = 1 − P(B|A)\`, used for complements.
  > Not a valid relationship.
- \`P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B)\`, used for joint distributions.
  > The joint probability is the numerator before dividing by \`P(B)\`.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When would you model something as Poisson rather than Binomial?

- When the probability of any individual success exceeds 0.5.
  > The success probability is not the criterion.
- [o] When events arrive at a rate with no fixed number of trials.
  > Support tickets per hour and defects per metre both fit. Binomial converges to Poisson when n is large and p is small, which is why the two so often approximate each other.
- When trials are dependent.
  > Both assume independence.
- When the outcome is continuous.
  > Both are discrete count distributions.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Define expectation and variance in one line each.

- Expectation is the most likely value; variance is the range.
  > The most likely value is the mode, and range is max minus min.
- Expectation is the median; variance is the interquartile range.
  > Those are robust alternatives, not definitions.
- Expectation is the sum of outcomes; variance is their product.
  > Neither is weighted by probability.
- [o] A probability-weighted average, and the spread around it.
  > Expectation is the weighted average of the outcomes; variance is the expected squared deviation from it. Variance being in squared units is exactly why the standard deviation is what gets reported.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Two dice are rolled. What is the probability the sum is 7?

- 1/12, one for each ordered pair.
  > This undercounts the ways to make 7.
- 7/36.
  > Seven is the target sum, not the count of ways to reach it.
- 1/36.
  > That is the probability of one specific pair.
- [o] 6/36 = 1/6.
  > Six of the 36 equally likely ordered pairs sum to 7, which is more than any other total, the reason 7 is the modal sum.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`A bet pays \`+100\` with probability 0.3 and loses \`40\` with probability 0.7. Should you take it?

- No; the expected value is negative.
  > It is positive by 2.
- [o] Yes on expectation, since it works out at +2 per trial.
  > \`0.3 × 100 − 0.7 × 40 = +2\`. Variance and how often you can repeat it decide whether it is actually a good idea. A positive expectation with a bankroll too small to survive the variance is still a bad bet.
- No; you lose more often than you win.
  > Frequency of winning alone does not determine the expected value.
- It cannot be evaluated without knowing the number of trials.
  > Expected value per trial is well defined; the trial count affects the risk, not the sign.`}
/>

## Machine learning

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does the bias–variance trade-off describe?

- The trade between features and observations.
  > Related to the curse of dimensionality, but not this decomposition.
- [o] Model flexibility trades one source of error for the other.
  > Simple models underfit with high bias and low variance; flexible ones do the reverse, and total error balances the two. More data reduces variance without reducing bias, which is why a badly underfit model does not improve with a larger sample.
- The trade-off between total training time and final accuracy.
  > Compute cost is a separate axis.
- The trade between precision and recall.
  > That is a threshold trade-off on a single model.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What most reliably indicates overfitting?

- A large number of features.
  > Many features raise the risk without demonstrating it.
- A validation loss that sits below the training loss throughout.
  > That usually indicates regularization such as dropout being active only at training time.
- [o] A large and growing gap between training and validation performance.
  > The gap is the signal; the absolute level is not. Regularization, more data, or a simpler model closes it.
- Training accuracy above 95%.
  > High training accuracy is fine if validation matches it.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do L1 and L2 regularization differ in effect?

- L1 penalizes large weights and L2 penalizes small ones.
  > Both penalize magnitude; the shape of the penalty differs.
- [o] L1 zeroes coefficients outright; L2 only shrinks them.
  > So lasso performs feature selection and ridge does not. L2 handles correlated predictors more gracefully; L1 picks one somewhat arbitrarily. Elastic net blends both.
- L2 is used for classification and L1 for regression.
  > Both apply to either.
- L1 is always preferable with many features.
  > It is preferable when you expect genuine sparsity.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When do you favour recall over precision?

- When a false positive is by far the more expensive error.
  > That argues for precision.
- [o] When missing a positive case is the expensive error.
  > Disease screening, fraud detection, and safety alerts all qualify, especially when a follow-up step can filter false alarms. The threshold is a business decision, which is why reporting the full precision-recall curve is more useful than a single F1 number.
- When the model is a tree ensemble.
  > Model family is irrelevant.
- When the classes are balanced.
  > Balance does not determine which error matters more.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When is PR-AUC more informative than ROC-AUC?

- [o] Under heavy class imbalance, where ROC-AUC flatters.
  > A large number of true negatives keeps the false-positive rate low and the ROC curve optimistic. At 1% positives, a model flagging 100 cases to find 5 can still show an impressive ROC curve; the precision-recall view exposes it.
- When the dataset is small.
  > Size affects variance of both.
- When the classes are balanced.
  > ROC-AUC is perfectly informative there.
- When the model emits hard labels rather than probability scores.
  > Both require scores.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does k-fold cross-validation buy you?

- A larger training set.
  > Each fold trains on slightly less than the full data.
- A guarantee the model will generalize.
  > It estimates generalization on data resembling the sample.
- Protection against overfitting during the training run itself.
  > It measures generalization; it does not prevent overfitting.
- [o] An estimate averaged over k splits, plus its variability.
  > That is far less sensitive to one unlucky split than a single hold-out. For time series, ordinary k-fold leaks the future into the past; a rolling-origin split is the correct variant.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Where does data leakage most commonly creep in?

- Using a randomly drawn train and test split of the data.
  > A random split is standard for i.i.d. data; it leaks for time series.
- Using too many features.
  > Feature count alone does not leak.
- [o] Fitting a transform on all the data before splitting.
  > A scaler, imputer, or feature selector will all do it, as will a feature that encodes the outcome. A pipeline that fits every transform inside the training fold is the structural defence.
- Training for too many epochs.
  > That is overfitting, not leakage.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does bagging differ from boosting?

- Bagging applies only to trees.
  > It applies to any base learner.
- Bagging is sequential and boosting is parallel.
  > This has them the wrong way round.
- Boosting requires each of its base learners to be a strong one.
  > It deliberately uses weak learners.
- [o] One averages independent models, the other corrects in sequence.
  > Bagging trains on bootstrap samples and averages to cut variance; boosting trains sequentially, each model correcting its predecessor, mainly cutting bias. Boosting reaches higher accuracy more often and is far easier to overfit, which is why its learning rate and depth need care.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What makes a Random Forest more than just bagged decision trees?

- It relies on an entirely different splitting criterion.
  > The criterion is the same.
- It prunes each tree aggressively.
  > Individual trees are typically grown deep.
- [o] It samples a random subset of features at every split.
  > That decorrelates the trees, so averaging them cuts variance more effectively. Without that, bootstrap samples alone produce trees that all latch onto the same dominant feature.
- It boosts the trees sequentially.
  > It does not boost at all.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When is feature scaling necessary?

- Only for classification problems.
  > It applies to regression too.
- [o] For distance-based and gradient-based methods.
  > kNN, SVM, k-means, PCA, regularized linear models, and neural networks all qualify, because feature magnitude affects the objective or the geometry. Regularization is easy to forget here: an unscaled feature is penalized differently purely because of its units.
- For every model.
  > Trees are scale-invariant.
- Only when the features are correlated with each other.
  > Correlation is a separate issue.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How should you handle severe class imbalance?

- Use plain accuracy as the metric, so evaluation stays simple.
  > Accuracy is the metric imbalance breaks most thoroughly.
- Always oversample the minority class to 50/50.
  > Forcing balance distorts the base rate and can hurt calibration.
- Drop the minority class.
  > That removes the thing being predicted.
- [o] Pick a metric that survives imbalance, then tune from there.
  > Class weights, resampling, and threshold tuning are all options, each validated against the actual decision cost. Often the most useful move is adjusting the decision threshold on a well-calibrated model rather than touching the training data.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How should a high-cardinality categorical feature such as postcode be encoded?

- One-hot encoding, always.
  > Thousands of columns makes the matrix sparse and unwieldy.
- Dropping the feature.
  > Geography is usually informative; the issue is representation.
- Label encoding using integers assigned arbitrarily.
  > It imposes a false ordering that linear models and distance metrics will act on.
- [o] Target or frequency encoding fitted inside the folds.
  > An embedding is the other option. Target encoding leaks if it is fitted on all the data. Grouping rare levels into an "other" bucket is often the simplest effective step.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does PCA do?

- Selects the most predictive original features.
  > It creates new features rather than selecting existing ones.
- Normalizes every input feature to zero mean and unit variance.
  > That is a preprocessing step PCA requires, not what it does.
- Removes correlated features.
  > It transforms correlation into orthogonal components rather than deleting features.
- [o] Projects onto orthogonal directions of maximum variance.
  > Dimensionality falls at the cost of interpretability. It is unsupervised, so a low-variance direction it discards may be exactly the one that predicts the target.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does logistic regression produce a probability?

- By counting the proportion of positives among similar training rows.
  > That is closer to kNN.
- By normalizing the output across classes.
  > That is softmax, the multi-class generalization.
- [o] By squashing a linear combination through the logistic function.
  > It maps any real number into (0, 1). The coefficients are therefore log-odds: a one-unit change multiplies the odds by e raised to the coefficient.
- By fitting a straight line and clipping it to [0, 1].
  > Clipping would produce flat regions and a discontinuous gradient.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does it mean for a model to be well calibrated?

- [o] Among cases it assigns a 70% probability, about 70% are actually positive.
  > It matters whenever the score feeds a downstream decision with a cost: expected-value ranking, pricing, triage. Platt scaling and isotonic regression are the usual fixes.
- Its accuracy is above a threshold.
  > Accuracy says nothing about probability quality.
- It has been trained on balanced classes.
  > Rebalancing usually *decalibrates* a model.
- Its predictions are spread evenly across the [0, 1] range.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you design an A/B test for a new feature?

<MultipleChoice

/>

  markdown={`How would you estimate a market's size on the spot?

- [o] Build it up from population and state every assumption.
  > Narrow by the fraction plausibly in the segment, then apply a usage rate and a price. Being roughly right and explicit beats a precise number nobody can interrogate. Sanity-check the total against something known.
- Recall a published figure.
  > Interviewers are testing the reasoning, not recall.
- Say that it cannot be estimated without more data.
  > Estimating under uncertainty is the skill being assessed.
- Multiply the current user base by ten.
  > An arbitrary multiplier is not an estimate.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you compute each group's share of the overall total in pandas?

- \`df.groupby("g")["x"].sum() / df["x"].sum()\`
  > This works for a per-group result, but it does not align back to the original rows.
- [o] Divide by \`df.groupby("g")["x"].transform("sum")\`.
  > That gives a within-group share; divide by \`df["x"].sum()\` for a share of the overall total. \`transform\` is what broadcasts the group total back to each row so the division aligns.
- \`df["x"].pct_change()\`
  > That computes period-over-period change, not a share.
- \`df.groupby("g")["x"].apply(lambda s: s / s.sum())\`
  > It produces the right numbers with a multi-level index that usually needs untangling afterwards.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is method chaining, and why do data scientists favour it?

- [o] Expressing a transformation as one pipeline of calls.
  > It avoids naming a dozen intermediate frames and reads top to bottom. \`assign\`, \`pipe\`, and \`query\` exist largely to keep operations chainable; the cost is that debugging a long chain is harder.
- Chaining indexers such as \`df[...][...]\`.
  > That is chained *indexing*, the thing that triggers \`SettingWithCopyWarning\`.
- Calling several methods on different objects in sequence.
  > The chain operates on one flowing result.
- A way of parallelizing pandas operations.
  > No concurrency is involved.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you find users active in one period but not the next?

- \`INNER JOIN\` the two periods.
  > An inner join keeps exactly the users active in both.
- \`UNION\` the two periods together and count duplicates.
  > This identifies overlap rather than the difference.
- [o] \`LEFT JOIN\` and keep rows where the later side is \`NULL\`.
  > \`NOT EXISTS\` expresses the same anti-join. It is the anti-join pattern, and it is the basis of any churn definition.
- \`FULL OUTER JOIN\` with no filter.
  > That returns everyone from both periods.`}
