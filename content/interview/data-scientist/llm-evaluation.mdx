When a company ships features built on large language models, someone has to
answer "is it any good, and is the new version better?". That is a measurement
problem, which makes it a data scientist's problem, and interviews now ask it
directly. The statistics are the ones you already know (sampling error,
agreement, power, bias); what is new is applying them to outputs that are text,
vary run to run, and are often graded by another model.

## Q&A

### How have Data Scientist interviews changed with AI?

The core loop is the same: statistics, experimentation, SQL, product sense.
What has been layered on top, especially at mid and senior levels:

- **Evaluating LLM features**: designing an eval set, choosing metrics for
  open-ended output, and deciding whether an automated judge can be trusted.
- **Experimenting on AI features**, where the treatment is itself random.
- **LLM-assisted workflow questions**: how you would validate SQL or Python an
  assistant wrote, and how you would stop hallucinated "insights" from
  reaching an automated report.
- **AI-on and AI-off rounds.** Some companies allow an assistant in coding
  rounds and grade how you verify its output; others prohibit it. Ask the
  recruiter, round by round. The [AI-assisted coding](/interview-prep/backend-engineer/ai-assisted-coding)
  page covers both formats.

### How would you measure the quality of an LLM feature, such as drafted support replies?

Start from the decision, not the metric. Agree with stakeholders on what a good
reply is (correct, complete, on-policy, right tone) and turn that into a
rubric with pass/fail criteria. Then:

1. **Sample inputs from real traffic**, stratified by intent and difficulty so
   rare but important cases are present. A synthetic set written by the team
   tends to be easier than production.
2. **Get expert labels** on a subset against the rubric, with two raters on
   some items so you can measure how much humans agree with each other.
3. **Score candidate versions on the same items**, which makes the comparison
   paired and far more sensitive than comparing two separate samples.
4. **Report uncertainty**: a pass rate without a confidence interval invites
   people to ship noise.

### How large does an eval set need to be?

Large enough that the difference you care about exceeds the sampling error.
A pass rate is a proportion, so its 95% interval is roughly
$\pm 1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$:

With 100 items, 80% means "somewhere between about 72% and 88%", so a
prompt change that moves the score by three points is invisible. Two habits
help more than brute size. **Pair the comparison**: run both versions on the
same items and test only the items where they disagree (McNemar's test), which
removes the item-to-item variance. And **stratify**, so a regression on a small
but important slice is not averaged away.

### How do you know whether an LLM judge can be trusted?

Treat the judge as a measurement instrument and calibrate it. Have experts
label a sample, run the judge on the same items, and measure agreement,
**corrected for chance**. Raw agreement flatters a judge when one label
dominates. Cohen's kappa corrects for that:

$$
\kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}
$$

where $p_o$ is observed agreement and $p_e$ is the agreement expected if both
raters labeled at random with their own label frequencies. Suppose judge and
expert each mark 90% of replies "pass" and agree on 90% of items. Then
$p_e = 0.9^2 + 0.1^2 = 0.82$ and $\kappa = 0.08 / 0.18 \approx 0.44$: moderate
agreement, and far less impressive than "90%" sounds. Most of that agreement
comes from both saying "pass" by default.

Also test for the known biases (preference for the first answer shown, for
longer answers, and for the judge's own model family) and compare the judge's
agreement with the experts to the experts' agreement with each other. A judge
cannot be expected to beat the humans it imitates.

### How do you A/B test an LLM feature?

The usual machinery applies, with four adjustments:

- **Randomize by user, and keep it sticky** across sessions and conversations.
  If one user sees both arms, their experiences contaminate each other.
- **Expect more variance.** The same input can produce different outputs, so
  the treatment is a distribution, not a fixed change. Estimate variance from
  an A/A test or a pilot, and size the test from that rather than from a
  textbook formula with a guessed variance.
- **Pin the model version.** A provider can update a model under the same name
  during your test, which changes the treatment midway. Log the model version on
  every request.
- **Guardrails beyond the usual**: latency at the 95th percentile, cost per
  active user, refusal rate, and escalations to a human. An answer that is
  better but twice as slow, or twice as expensive, can be a net loss.

Offline evals and online tests answer different questions. The eval says
whether the output is better by your rubric; the experiment says whether users
are better off. Use the first to decide what is worth testing with the second.

### Why is a thumbs-up rate a weak primary metric?

Few users click either button, and the ones who do are not representative, so
the rate is dominated by selection bias. It also rewards what feels good in the
moment: confident tone and agreement with the user, not correctness. Prefer
**downstream behavior**: the ticket was resolved without reopening, the
suggested code was still in the file a week later, the user did not rephrase
and ask again. Keep explicit feedback as a diagnostic, not the target.

### You use an LLM to classify a million support tickets. How do you report the share that are complaints?

Not as the raw share the model labels "complaint", because the classifier's
errors bias it. Validate it on a hand-labeled sample to estimate its
**sensitivity** (share of true complaints it catches) and **specificity**
(share of non-complaints it correctly leaves alone). The observed rate is then

$$
p_{\text{obs}} = \text{sens} \cdot \pi + (1 - \text{spec})(1 - \pi)
$$

for true prevalence $\pi$, which solves to

$$
\pi = \frac{p_{\text{obs}} + \text{spec} - 1}{\text{sens} + \text{spec} - 1}
$$

(the Rogan–Gladen estimator). With sensitivity 0.90, specificity 0.95, and 20%
of tickets labeled complaints, the corrected estimate is about 17.6%: the
false positives from the large non-complaint majority had inflated the count.
Report the interval too, since sensitivity and specificity are themselves
estimated from a sample.

### How do you check an analysis an AI assistant wrote for you?

As you would check a junior colleague's notebook, with extra suspicion in the
places assistants tend to slip:

- **Silent data loss**: a `dropna()` or an inner join that quietly removed a
  third of the rows. Compare row counts at each step.
- **Leakage**: a scaler or encoder fit on the full dataset before the split,
  or a feature computed from the future.
- **Test choice**: a t-test on heavily skewed revenue, or twenty segment
  comparisons with no correction for multiple testing.
- **Invented fields or functions**: a column that does not exist, or a library
  call with the wrong arguments.
- **The narrative**: causal language ("the redesign drove growth") that the
  analysis cannot support.

Then reproduce the headline number by an independent route, such as a second
query, a different tool, or a total someone else already trusts.

### What is benchmark contamination, and why does it matter to you?

If a public benchmark's test items appeared in a model's training data, its
score measures memory rather than skill. It is data leakage at the scale of the
internet. The practical consequences: do not choose a model on public leaderboard
numbers alone, keep your own eval set private, refresh it with recent real cases,
and never feed it into fine-tuning or few-shot prompts.

  markdown={`Version B of a prompt scores 82% on a 100-item eval set; version A scored 79% on the same items. What is the most defensible conclusion?

- B is better and should ship, because it scored higher.
  > With 100 items, the uncertainty on each score is around 8 points, which swamps a 3-point gap.
- A is better, because small eval sets favor the older version.
  > There is no such systematic effect; the data simply cannot separate them.
- [o] The difference is within sampling noise; test the items where they disagree, or add items.
  > A paired test on the discordant items (McNemar) is the sensitive comparison, and more items narrow the interval.
- The eval set is useless and should be replaced with a public benchmark.
  > Public benchmarks measure a different task and may be contaminated; a larger private set is the fix.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`An LLM judge agrees with expert raters on 92% of items, but both mark about 95% of items "pass". What should you compute before trusting the judge?

- The judge's average score across all items.
  > The average says nothing about whether it agrees with experts on the items that matter.
- [o] A chance-corrected agreement such as Cohen's kappa.
  > When one label dominates, two raters agree often by chance. Kappa subtracts that and here would be low.
- The judge's accuracy on a public benchmark.
  > General capability does not show agreement with your experts on your rubric.
- Nothing more, since 92% agreement is already high.
  > With 95% of items "pass", two raters labeling at random would already agree about 90% of the time, so 92% is barely better than chance.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Midway through an A/B test of an AI assistant, the provider silently updates the model behind the same API name. What is the main threat to the experiment?

- The sample ratio between arms will become unbalanced.
  > Assignment is unchanged; users stay in their arms.
- Users will notice and churn, causing a novelty effect.
  > Possible, but secondary to the treatment itself changing.
- The control group is contaminated by the new model.
  > The control arm does not use the assistant, so it is unaffected.
