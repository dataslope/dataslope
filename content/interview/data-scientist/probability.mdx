Probability underpins every statistics and modeling question, so
interviewers probe it directly, both the definitions (expectation,
variance, common distributions) and the puzzles (Monty Hall, birthday)
that reveal whether you can reason about uncertainty. Review the Q&A, run
the simulations, then take the concept checks.

## Q&A

### What is conditional probability?

$P(A \mid B)$ is the probability of $A$ given that $B$ has occurred:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$$

It renormalizes the sample space to the world where $B$ is true. Much of
applied probability is bookkeeping about which events you are conditioning
on.

### State Bayes' theorem and explain when you would reach for it.

Bayes' theorem inverts a conditional, turning $P(B \mid A)$ into
$P(A \mid B)$:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A) + P(B \mid \neg A)P(\neg A)$$

Reach for it whenever you know a likelihood ("given the disease, the test
is 99% positive") but want a posterior ("given a positive test, how likely
is the disease?"). The denominator (the total probability of the evidence)
is where the base rate sneaks in and where most people's intuition breaks.

### Why does a 99%-accurate test for a rare disease still produce mostly false positives?

Because the base rate dominates. With 1% prevalence, 99% sensitivity, and a
5% false-positive rate, the many healthy people generate far more false
positives than the few sick people generate true positives. The code walks
the arithmetic: the posterior comes out near 17%, not 99%.

### What does it mean for two events to be independent?

$A$ and $B$ are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the
other: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$, equivalently $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$.
Independence is an assumption you should justify, not assume; correlated
failures, repeated measurements on the same user, and network effects all
break it.

### Independent vs. mutually exclusive, what's the difference?

**Mutually exclusive** events cannot co-occur, so $P(A \cap B) = 0$.
**Independent** events satisfy $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. They are nearly
opposites: two events with non-zero probability that are mutually exclusive
are *never* independent, because observing one drives the other's
probability to zero.

### Define expectation and variance.

**Expectation** is the probability-weighted average of a random variable,
$E[X] = \sum_x x\,P(X = x)$ (or $\int x f(x)\,dx$ in the continuous case).
**Variance** measures spread around that mean,
$\text{Var}(X) = E[(X - E[X])^2] = E[X^2] - E[X]^2$. The code computes both
for a fair die directly from the definition, $E[X] = 3.5$ and
$\text{Var}(X) = 35/12 \approx 2.917$.

### What is linearity of expectation, and why is it so useful?

$E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y]$ **for any** $X$ and $Y$, even when they are
dependent. That "even when dependent" is the whole point: it lets you break
a messy count into indicator variables and sum their probabilities. For
example, the expected number of fixed points in a random permutation is
$n \cdot \frac{1}{n} = 1$, computed without touching the joint
distribution. Variance, by contrast, only adds for **independent** (or
uncorrelated) variables.

### Walk through the common distributions a data scientist should know.

- **Bernoulli($p$)**, a single yes/no trial; mean $p$, variance $p(1-p)$.
- **Binomial($n, p$)**, the count of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli
  trials; mean $np$, variance $np(1-p)$.
- **Poisson($\lambda$)**, counts of rare events in a fixed interval; mean
  and variance both $\lambda$.
- **Normal($\mu, \sigma^2$)**, the bell curve the CLT drives everything
  toward; symmetric, defined by mean and variance.
- **Exponential($\lambda$)**, the waiting time between Poisson events; mean
  $1/\lambda$, and memoryless.

### What does "the exponential distribution is memoryless" mean?

The future is independent of the past: $P(X > s + t \mid X > s) = P(X > t)$.
A component that has already survived $s$ hours has the same remaining-life
distribution as a brand-new one. The geometric distribution is its discrete
analogue, and the exponential is the *only* continuous distribution with
this property.

### When would you model something as Poisson vs. Binomial?

Use **Binomial** when there is a fixed number of independent trials each
with the same success probability (10 coin flips, 1,000 impressions with a
click probability). Use **Poisson** when you are counting events over a
continuous interval with no natural upper bound and a constant rate,
arrivals per minute, defects per wafer. The Poisson is the limit of the
Binomial as $n \to \infty$, $p \to 0$ with $np = \lambda$ fixed.

### What is the Law of Large Numbers?

As the sample size grows, the sample mean converges to the true mean
$\mu$. It is why more data yields more reliable estimates. The simulation
rolls a die 100,000 times and tracks the running average, which drifts from
a noisy early value toward $3.5$.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
rolls = rng.integers(1, 7, size=100_000)
running_mean = np.cumsum(rolls) / np.arange(1, len(rolls) + 1)
print(f"mean after 10:     {running_mean[9]:.3f}")
print(f"mean after 1,000:  {running_mean[999]:.3f}")
print(f"mean after 100k:   {running_mean[-1]:.3f}")
print("True mean is 3.5")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

### What is the Central Limit Theorem, and how does it differ from the LLN?

The CLT says the distribution of the **sample mean** approaches a normal
curve as $n$ grows, *regardless of the population's shape*, with standard
deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. The LLN tells you **where** the sample mean
lands ($\mu$); the CLT tells you **what shape** its uncertainty takes
(normal). The simulation draws 5,000 sample means from a heavily skewed
exponential population; their histogram is nearly symmetric even though the
raw data is not.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
population = rng.exponential(scale=2.0, size=200_000)  # skewed
means = np.array([rng.choice(population, 60).mean() for _ in range(5000)])

print(f"population skew is strong; mean={population.mean():.3f}")
print(f"sample-mean std (simulated): {means.std():.3f}")
print(f"sample-mean std (formula):   {population.std() / 60**0.5:.3f}")
`,
    },
  ]}

### Explain the Monty Hall problem.

You pick one of three doors; the host, who knows where the car is, opens a
different door revealing a goat, then offers a switch. **You should
switch**, it wins with probability $2/3$ versus $1/3$ for staying. The key
insight: your original pick is right only $1/3$ of the time, and the host's
informed reveal transfers the remaining $2/3$ onto the single unopened
door. Switching wins exactly when your first guess was wrong.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
trials = 100_000
car = rng.integers(0, 3, trials)
first_pick = rng.integers(0, 3, trials)

# Switching wins exactly when the first pick was NOT the car.
stay_win = (first_pick == car).mean()
switch_win = (first_pick != car).mean()
print(f"Stay wins:   {stay_win:.3f}  (~1/3)")
print(f"Switch wins: {switch_win:.3f}  (~2/3)")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

### Explain the birthday problem.

In a room of just 23 people, the probability that two share a birthday
exceeds 50%, far lower than intuition suggests. The reason: there are
$\binom{23}{2} = 253$ *pairs*, and it is pairs, not people, that can
collide. The exact probability is
$1 - \frac{365}{365}\cdot\frac{364}{365}\cdots\frac{343}{365}$. The
simulation confirms it empirically.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
trials, n = 50_000, 23
days = rng.integers(0, 365, size=(trials, n))
days.sort(axis=1)
has_match = (np.diff(days, axis=1) == 0).any(axis=1)
print(f"P(shared birthday, n=23) = {has_match.mean():.3f}")
print("Exact answer is about 0.507")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

### What is the difference between covariance and correlation?

**Covariance** measures how two variables move together,
$\text{Cov}(X, Y) = E[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)]$, but its magnitude depends
on the variables' units. **Correlation** normalizes it to
$[-1, 1]$: $\rho = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \sigma_Y}$, making it
scale-free and comparable across variable pairs. Zero correlation means no
*linear* relationship, not independence.

### How do you compute the expected value of a simple bet?

Weight each payoff by its probability and sum. A bet that pays \$10 with
probability $0.2$ and loses \$3 otherwise has expected value
$0.2 \times 10 + 0.8 \times (-3) = 2 - 2.4 = -0.4$, so it loses 40 cents per
play on average. Interviewers use these to check that you distinguish
expected value from the most likely outcome, and that you can spot a
negative-EV proposition.
