Analysts are judged as much on **judgment and communication** as on
query syntax. These come up in the "how would you analyze…?" portion of
the interview.

## Q&A

### When would you report the median instead of the mean?

When the distribution is **skewed** or has outliers. Income, house
prices, and session durations are right-skewed: a few huge values drag
the mean up, so the median ("typical" value) is more representative.
Report both when the gap between them is itself informative.

### Explain "correlation does not imply causation" with an example.

Two variables can move together because of a **confounder** or
coincidence, not because one causes the other. Ice-cream sales correlate
with drownings — both are driven by hot weather (the confounder), not by
each other. To argue causation you need a controlled experiment (A/B
test) or a careful causal-inference design.

### What is sampling bias, and how can it distort results?

When the sample isn't representative of the population, conclusions don't
generalize. Surveying only app users about a feature ignores people who
churned because of it (**survivorship bias**); a web poll captures only
the engaged (**self-selection**). The fix is sampling design — random or
stratified sampling — not more data.

### A metric went up 20% week-over-week. How do you investigate?

State assumptions, then decompose: (1) is it real or an artifact
(tracking change, bot traffic, a deploy)? (2) segment it — which
region/platform/cohort drove it? (3) check seasonality / day-of-week and
compare to the same period last year; (4) look for a denominator change
(did traffic drop, inflating a rate?). Conclude with the most likely
driver **and** what you'd verify next.

### How do you present a finding to a non-technical stakeholder?

Lead with the **answer and its business impact**, not the method. One
clear chart over a table; label the axes and the takeaway. State
assumptions and caveats plainly ("this excludes refunds"), and end with a
recommended action. Save methodology for an appendix or backup slide.

### What are variance and standard deviation, and what do they measure?

**Variance** is the average squared deviation from the mean,
$\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2$. **Standard
deviation** is its square root, $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$, returned to
the original units. Both measure the **spread** of a distribution: a
higher value means data points are more dispersed around the mean.
Standard deviation is easier to interpret because it shares units with
the data (e.g., dollars, not dollars-squared).

The snippet below builds a `pd.Series` and calls `.var()` and `.std()`,
which return the **sample** variance and standard deviation (`ddof=1`)
that divide by $n - 1$ rather than $n$.

### What is the mode, and when is it the most useful measure of center?

The **mode** is the most frequently occurring value. It is most useful
for **categorical** or discrete data (e.g., the most common product
category purchased, the most frequent support ticket type) where mean
and median have no clear meaning.

### What is a normal distribution and why does it matter for analysts?

A normal (Gaussian) distribution is bell-shaped and symmetric, fully
described by its mean and standard deviation. Many natural phenomena
approximate it, and it underpins common statistical tests. In practice,
analysts care because departures from normality (heavy tails, skew) can
invalidate assumptions of parametric tests.

### What is the difference between a population and a sample?

The **population** is the full set of observations you care about (e.g.,
all customers). A **sample** is a subset drawn from it. We analyze
samples because measuring the full population is usually impractical.
Sample statistics (mean, proportion) are **estimates** of the true
population parameters, subject to sampling error.

### What are percentiles and quartiles?

A **percentile** is the value below which a given percentage of
observations fall: the 90th percentile means 90% of values are below
that point. **Quartiles** divide data into four equal parts: $Q_1$
(25th), $Q_2$ (50th = median), $Q_3$ (75th). The interquartile range
($\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$) is a robust measure of spread.

The snippet below uses `Series.quantile` to pull $Q_1$ and $Q_3$,
derives the IQR as their difference, and also reads off the 90th
percentile.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import pandas as pd

s = pd.Series([10, 20, 30, 40, 50])
q1, q3 = s.quantile(0.25), s.quantile(0.75)
iqr = q3 - q1
p90 = s.quantile(0.90)
print(f"Q1={q1}, Q3={q3}, IQR={iqr}, 90th pct={p90}")`,
    },
  ]}

### How do you identify outliers?

Common methods:
- **IQR rule**: values below $Q_1 - 1.5\times\text{IQR}$ or above $Q_3 + 1.5\times\text{IQR}$ are flagged as outliers.
- **Z-score**: values whose $z = \frac{x - \bar{x}}{s}$ exceeds 2-3 in absolute value, i.e. more than 2-3 standard deviations from the mean.
- **Visual**: box plots or scatter plots reveal extreme points quickly.

Always investigate outliers before removing them — they may be data
errors or genuinely important observations.

The snippet below applies both rules to the `amount` column: it builds
the IQR fences to flag `outliers_iqr`, then computes a z-score per row
and flags `outliers_z` where the absolute z-score exceeds 2.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "amount": [10, 12, 11, 13, 9, 14, 10, 12, 200, 8, 11, 13, 190]
})`,
      starterCode: `q1, q3 = df["amount"].quantile([0.25, 0.75])
iqr = q3 - q1
outliers_iqr = df[(df["amount"] < q1 - 1.5 * iqr) |
                  (df["amount"] > q3 + 1.5 * iqr)]
print("IQR outliers:", outliers_iqr["amount"].tolist())

z_scores = (df["amount"] - df["amount"].mean()) / df["amount"].std()
outliers_z = df[z_scores.abs() > 2]
print("Z-score outliers:", outliers_z["amount"].tolist())`,
    },
  ]}
/>

### What is the purpose of an A/B test?

An A/B test is a **controlled experiment** that randomly assigns users to
a control group (A) and a treatment group (B) to measure the causal
effect of a change (e.g., a new button color) on a metric. Randomization
ensures the groups are comparable, so any difference in outcomes can be
attributed to the treatment.

### What is a p-value in the context of an A/B test?

The p-value is the probability of observing a result at least as extreme
as the one measured, **if the null hypothesis (no difference) were true**.
A small p-value (commonly below 0.05) suggests the result is unlikely
under the null, providing evidence to reject it. It does **not** measure
the probability that the null is true or the effect's practical size.

### What is statistical significance vs. practical significance?

**Statistical significance** means the observed effect is unlikely to be
due to chance (low p-value). **Practical significance** means the effect
is large enough to matter in the real world. A very large sample can make
a tiny, meaningless difference statistically significant. Always report
effect size alongside p-values.

### What is a Type I and a Type II error?

- **Type I error (false positive)**: you reject the null hypothesis when
  it is actually true — concluding the treatment works when it doesn't.
  Controlled by the significance level (alpha, often 0.05).
- **Type II error (false negative)**: you fail to reject the null when it
  is actually false — missing a real effect. Controlled by statistical
  power (typically 80%).

|                          | $H_0$ is true                                | $H_0$ is false                              |
| ------------------------ | -------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **Reject $H_0$**         | Type I error (false positive) — rate $\alpha$ | Correct decision — power $= 1 - \beta$       |
| **Fail to reject $H_0$** | Correct decision — rate $1 - \alpha$          | Type II error (false negative) — rate $\beta$ |

### What is statistical power?

Power is the probability of correctly detecting a real effect (rejecting
the null when it is false), defined as $1 - \beta$ where $\beta$ is the
Type II error rate. Power increases with larger sample sizes, larger
effect sizes, and higher significance levels. A common target is 80%
power. Low-powered tests risk missing real improvements.

### How do you choose the right sample size for an A/B test?

Use a power calculation based on: the minimum detectable effect (the
smallest change worth caring about), the baseline metric value, the
desired power (commonly 80%), and the significance level (commonly 5%).
Larger samples shrink the standard error of the estimate,
$SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$, which is what makes a given effect easier
to detect. Online calculators and Python's `statsmodels` can compute the
required size before running the test.

The snippet below uses `statsmodels`' `NormalIndPower` and its
`solve_power` method to back out the required sample size per group for a
0.1 effect size at 80% power and a 5% significance level.

### What is the difference between correlation and the correlation coefficient?

**Correlation** is a general term for the linear association between two
variables. The **Pearson correlation coefficient** ($r$) quantifies it on
the range $-1 \le r \le 1$ — from $-1$ (perfect negative) to $+1$
(perfect positive), with 0 meaning no linear relationship. Spearman rank
correlation is used when the relationship is monotonic but not linear, or
when data is ordinal.

The snippet below calls `Series.corr` to get the Pearson $r$ between
`price` and `sales`, builds a full `corr()` matrix over three columns,
and then switches to `method="spearman"` for the rank-based coefficient.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import pandas as pd

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "price":    [10, 20, 30, 40, 50],
    "sales":    [200, 160, 120, 80, 60],
    "ad_spend": [5, 8, 15, 22, 30],
})`,
      starterCode: `# Pearson r between two columns
r = df["price"].corr(df["sales"])
print(f"Pearson r: {r:.3f}")

# Full correlation matrix
corr_matrix = df[["price", "sales", "ad_spend"]].corr()
print(corr_matrix.round(3))

# Spearman (rank-based)
r_spearman = df["price"].corr(df["sales"], method="spearman")
print(f"Spearman r: {r_spearman:.3f}")`,
    },
  ]}

### What chart type would you choose for each scenario?

- **Trend over time** — line chart.
- **Comparing categories** — bar chart (or horizontal bar for many categories).
- **Part-of-whole** — stacked bar chart; pie chart only for 2-4 slices.
- **Distribution of a continuous variable** — histogram or box plot.
- **Relationship between two continuous variables** — scatter plot.
- **Heatmap** — correlation matrix or pivot-style table with color encoding.

### How do you choose which metric to track for a product change?

Define a **primary metric** that directly captures the goal (e.g.,
conversion rate for a checkout change), plus **guardrail metrics** that
must not degrade (e.g., refund rate, session duration). Avoid vanity
metrics that look good but don't tie to business outcomes. Make sure the
metric is measurable, attributable to the change, and sensitive enough to
detect the expected effect size.

### What is the difference between a dashboard and a report?

A **dashboard** is a living view updated continuously (or on a schedule)
for ongoing monitoring — it answers "what is happening now?" A **report**
is typically a one-time or periodic document that answers a specific
question, includes context and narrative, and is consumed linearly.
Dashboards are for operational decisions; reports are for deeper analysis
and stakeholder communication.

### What makes a dashboard effective?

Keep it to the key metrics decision-makers act on. Group related key
performance indicators (KPIs).
Use consistent scales and color conventions. Add clear titles and data
freshness labels. Avoid clutter: every element should earn its place.
Design for the audience — an executive view differs from an ops team view.

### How do you explain a complex statistical finding in plain language?

Anchor to a concrete, relatable number ("customers who saw the new
design were 12% more likely to purchase"). Avoid jargon; replace "95%
confidence interval" with "we're highly confident the true lift is between
8% and 16%." Use a visual. Tie it explicitly to the business decision it
informs.

### What is a pivot table in a spreadsheet and when do you use it?

A pivot table summarizes a flat data table by grouping rows into
categories and applying aggregations (sum, count, average) — the
spreadsheet equivalent of `GROUP BY`. Use it for quick exploratory
aggregation, cross-tabs, and reporting without writing code.

### What is VLOOKUP and what are its limitations?

`VLOOKUP(lookup_value, table_array, col_index, FALSE)` finds a value in
the first column of a range and returns the value from a specified column
in the same row. Limitations: it only looks **left-to-right**, is
fragile when columns are inserted, and is slower than index-match on
large datasets. `XLOOKUP` (modern Excel) removes most of these
restrictions.

### What does XLOOKUP improve over VLOOKUP?

`XLOOKUP` separates the lookup array from the return array, so it can
look in any direction (not just left-to-right), handles missing matches
with a built-in `if_not_found` argument, and is generally more readable.
It is available in Excel 365 and Excel 2021+.

### What is the difference between absolute and relative cell references in spreadsheets?

A **relative** reference (e.g., `A1`) adjusts when you copy a formula to
another cell. An **absolute** reference (e.g., `$A$1`) stays fixed.
Mixed references fix only the row (`A$1`) or only the column (`$A1`).
Use absolute references for constants like a tax rate that should not
shift when the formula is copied down a column.

### How do you communicate uncertainty in a data finding?

State your confidence explicitly ("based on the last 90 days of data"),
report ranges not just point estimates, name the main assumptions, and
quantify how sensitive the conclusion is to those assumptions. Avoid
false precision — rounding "12.37% lift" to "about 12%" is often more
honest when the confidence interval is wide.

### What is the difference between a histogram and a bar chart?

A **bar chart** compares discrete categories (each bar is one category).
A **histogram** shows the distribution of a **continuous** variable by
dividing the range into bins and counting how many observations fall in
each bin. The x-axis of a histogram is numeric and ordered; bar chart
categories can be in any order.

### What is Simpson's Paradox?

Simpson's Paradox occurs when a trend that appears in aggregate data
**reverses** or disappears when the data is broken into subgroups. For
example, a drug may appear less effective overall but be more effective
in every demographic subgroup, because sicker (harder-to-treat) patients
were disproportionately assigned to the treatment group. It is a reminder
to always segment data before drawing conclusions.

### What is a cohort analysis?

A cohort analysis tracks a group of users who share a common starting
event (e.g., sign-up date) over time. It isolates behavioral patterns
from the changing composition of the user base, making it the standard
tool for measuring retention, churn, and lifetime value by acquisition
cohort.
