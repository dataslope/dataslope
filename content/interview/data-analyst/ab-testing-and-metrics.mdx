Product analysts live in **metrics and experiments**: defining what to
measure, reading an A/B test without overclaiming, and knowing when an
aggregate number is lying to you. This page mixes Q&A, concept checks, and
a **runnable** funnel query.

## Q&A

### What makes a good metric?

A good metric is **specific, measurable, and tied to the outcome you
care about**, and sensitive enough to move when the product changes.
Prefer **rates** (conversion rate, retention rate) over raw counts,
because counts confound the effect with traffic volume. It should also be
hard to game: optimizing it shouldn't let you win the metric while losing
the business.

### What's the difference between a primary, guardrail, and vanity metric?

- **Primary (success) metric**, the one the change is trying to move
  (e.g., checkout conversion for a new checkout flow).
- **Guardrail metric**, something that must **not** get worse as a side
  effect (e.g., page latency, refund rate, unsubscribe rate).
- **Vanity metric**, looks impressive but doesn't tie to a decision
  (e.g., total registered users, raw pageviews).

You commit to the primary and guardrails **before** running the test.

### What is a north star metric?

A single metric that best captures the long-term value your product
delivers to users, and that teams align around, e.g., "weekly active
teams" for collaboration software. It sits above the tactical metrics and
keeps day-to-day optimization pointed at durable value rather than local
wins.

### What is a funnel, and how do you measure conversion at each step?

A funnel is an ordered sequence of steps a user must complete (view →
add-to-cart → checkout → purchase). Step conversion is the share of users
who reach the next step; overall conversion is the share who finish the
whole thing. Measuring per-step conversion pinpoints **where** users drop
off.

### How do you compute a conversion rate in SQL?

Count the users who reach a stage, divide by the users at the reference
stage. Watch two things: use `COUNT(DISTINCT user_id)` so repeat events
don't inflate the numerator, and cast to float (`* 1.0`) so integer
division doesn't truncate to 0.

```sql
SELECT
  COUNT(DISTINCT CASE WHEN stage = 'purchase' THEN user_id END) * 1.0
  / COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS overall_conversion
FROM events;
```

### What is a step-to-step conversion, and how does LAG help?

Step conversion divides each stage's users by the **previous** stage's,
revealing the single biggest drop-off. In SQL, `LAG(users)` over the
funnel order gives the prior stage's count on the same row:

```sql
SELECT stage, users,
  users * 1.0 / LAG(users) OVER (ORDER BY stage_order) AS step_conversion
FROM funnel;
```

### What is the purpose of an A/B test?

It's a **controlled experiment**: randomly assign users to control (A) and
treatment (B), change one thing, and compare a metric. Randomization
makes the groups comparable in expectation, so a difference in the metric
can be attributed to the change rather than to a confounder, this is how
you get **causation**, not just correlation.

### What is a p-value, at an analyst's level?

The probability of seeing a result **at least as extreme** as the one you
observed **if there were truly no difference** (the null hypothesis). A
small p-value (commonly < 0.05) means the data would be surprising under
"no effect," so you have evidence of a real difference. It is **not** the
probability that the null is true, nor the size or importance of the
effect.

### What's the difference between statistical and practical significance?

**Statistical** significance says an effect is unlikely to be noise.
**Practical** significance asks whether it's big enough to matter. With a
huge sample, a 0.1% lift can be statistically significant yet worthless in
practice. Always report the **effect size** (and its range) alongside the
p-value.

### How would you explain a confidence interval to a stakeholder?

Give a plain-language range instead of a bare point estimate: "we're
confident the true lift is between about **8% and 16%**." A 95% interval
means that if we repeated the experiment many times, about 95% of such
intervals would contain the true value. A **wide** interval signals you
don't yet have enough data to be precise.

### Why does sample size matter, and what is a minimum detectable effect?

Bigger samples shrink the standard error, so smaller true effects become
detectable. The **minimum detectable effect (MDE)** is the smallest lift
you want to be able to catch; you pick it up front, along with power
(often 80%) and significance (often 5%), to compute the required sample
size **before** launching. Underpowered tests miss real wins.

### What is "peeking," and why is it a problem?

Peeking is repeatedly checking an A/B test and stopping the moment it
looks significant. Because the metric fluctuates, early looks cross the
0.05 line by chance far more than 5% of the time, inflating false
positives. Fix it by fixing the sample size in advance, or use sequential
testing methods designed for continuous monitoring.

### What is a novelty (or primacy) effect?

A temporary change in behavior just because something is **new**, users
click a redesigned button out of curiosity, then revert. It can make an
early A/B readout look better (or worse) than the steady state. Guard
against it by running the test long enough and checking whether the effect
persists for later-joining users.

### What is Simpson's paradox?

A trend that holds in aggregate can **reverse** within every subgroup, or
vice versa, usually because of an unequal mix. A treatment can look worse
overall yet be better in each segment if it was applied more to
harder-to-serve users. The lesson: **segment before you conclude**, and
watch for confounding in the group sizes.

### What is a cohort analysis, and what does it reveal?

A cohort analysis groups users by a shared start event (e.g., sign-up
month) and tracks them over time. By holding the starting point fixed, it
separates genuine behavior changes from shifts in user-base composition,
making it the standard tool for **retention**, churn, and lifetime-value
comparisons across acquisition cohorts.

### What is a retention curve?

For a cohort, the percentage still active in week 0, week 1, week 2, and
so on. Healthy products show the curve **flattening** onto a stable
plateau (a retained core), rather than decaying toward zero. Comparing
curves across cohorts shows whether product changes actually improved
stickiness.

### How do you avoid a misleading ratio metric?

Be deliberate about the **denominator** and its grain. A rising "revenue
per session" can come from more revenue **or** fewer sessions, so watch
numerator and denominator together. Averages of ratios differ from the
ratio of totals; when in doubt, aggregate the numerator and denominator
separately, then divide.

## Concept checks

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`An A/B test on 2 million users shows a **0.05%** lift in clicks with a p-value of \`0.001\`. What's the right read?

- Ship it, a tiny p-value means a big, reliable win.
  > A small p-value only says the effect is unlikely to be pure noise; it says nothing about the effect's *size*.
- [o] It's statistically significant but likely not practically significant; weigh the 0.05% against costs.
  > Huge samples make trivial effects significant. Report the effect size and decide whether 0.05% is worth shipping.
- The test is broken because the lift is so small.
  > A small effect isn't a bug; with enough data it can be measured precisely yet still be too small to matter.
- A p-value of 0.001 means there's a 0.1% chance the null is true.
  > The p-value is computed *assuming* the null; it isn't the probability that the null is true.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Overall, treatment B has a lower conversion rate than A. But within **both** new users and returning users, B beats A. What's going on?

- The data is contradictory and must be wrong.
  > It's not a contradiction, it's a well-known statistical effect that arises from group mix.
- [o] Simpson's paradox: an unequal mix of segments across A and B reverses the aggregate.
  > If B has a larger share of the harder-to-convert segment, its blended rate can trail A even while winning every segment. Segment before concluding.
- B is genuinely worse; the subgroup results are a coincidence.
  > Winning in *every* subgroup isn't coincidence; the aggregate is misleading because of the differing segment proportions.
- You must have peeked at the results early.
  > Peeking inflates false positives over time; it doesn't explain a consistent sign reversal between aggregate and subgroups.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`A team ships a change to lift "add to cart" clicks, but "add to cart" is also their success metric. Refunds quietly rise. What was missing?

- Nothing; more add-to-cart clicks is always good.
  > Optimizing a single metric without protection let a harmful side effect (refunds) slip through.
- [o] A guardrail metric like refund rate that must not degrade while the primary metric improves.
  > Guardrails are defined up front to catch exactly this: a primary-metric win that damages the business elsewhere.
- A larger sample size.
  > More data wouldn't have surfaced the refund problem; watching the right *metric* would.
- A different chart type.
  > Visualization isn't the gap; the missing piece is a guardrail metric on the experiment.`}
/>
