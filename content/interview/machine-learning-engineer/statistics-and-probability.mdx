Statistics is where ML interviews check that you understand *why* a model
works, not just how to call it: estimation, uncertainty, and the difference
between signal and noise. Review the Q&A, run the simulations, then take
the concept checks and the challenge.

## Q&A

### What is a random variable, and what's the difference between a PMF and a PDF?

A random variable maps outcomes to numbers. A **discrete** random variable
has a probability **mass** function (PMF) giving $P(X = x)$ for each value;
a **continuous** one has a probability **density** function (PDF) where
probability is the *area under* the curve over an interval, and $P(X = x) = 0$
for any single point. Both integrate/sum to 1, and expectation is the
probability-weighted average of the values.

### Describe the distributions an ML engineer should know.

**Bernoulli/Binomial** for binary outcomes and counts of successes;
**Normal (Gaussian)** for symmetric continuous data and, via the CLT, for
sample means; **Poisson** for counts of rare events at a constant rate
($\text{mean} = \text{variance} = \lambda$); **Uniform** for equal-
probability outcomes; **Exponential** for waiting times between Poisson
events. Knowing each one's support, parameters, and mean/variance lets you
pick a sensible likelihood or prior.

### What is Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)?

MLE picks the parameters that make the observed data most probable under a
chosen model. Because products of probabilities underflow, you maximize the
**log-likelihood**:

$$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MLE}} = \arg\max_\theta \prod_i p(x_i \mid \theta) = \arg\max_\theta \sum_i \log p(x_i \mid \theta)$$

For a Gaussian, the MLE of the mean is just the sample mean; for logistic
regression, maximizing the log-likelihood of the labels *is* the training
objective. MLE is consistent and asymptotically efficient but can overfit
small samples.

### Explain the bias–variance decomposition.

Expected test error splits into three parts:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y - \hat{f}(x))^2\big] = \underbrace{\text{Bias}[\hat{f}(x)]^2}_{\text{underfitting}} + \underbrace{\text{Var}[\hat{f}(x)]}_{\text{overfitting}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}$$

**Bias** is error from wrong assumptions (too simple a model); **variance**
is sensitivity to the particular training sample; $\sigma^2$ is noise you
can't remove. More capacity lowers bias but raises variance, tuning
complexity and regularization minimizes their sum.

### What is the difference between standard deviation and standard error?

**Standard deviation** measures the spread of individual observations.
**Standard error** measures the spread of a *statistic* (usually the mean)
across hypothetical repeated samples:

$$SE = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$$

The SE shrinks as $n$ grows (you estimate the mean more precisely with more
data); the standard deviation estimates a fixed population property and
doesn't. Confusing the two is a classic interview slip.

### What does the Central Limit Theorem say, and why does it matter?

For a large enough sample, the distribution of the **sample mean** is
approximately normal **regardless of the population's shape**, with standard
deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. That's what justifies normal-based confidence
intervals and z/t-tests for means even on skewed data. The simulation below
draws thousands of sample means from a skewed exponential population and
shows the simulated SE matching the formula:

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
population = rng.exponential(scale=3.0, size=100_000)   # heavily skewed
sample_means = np.array([rng.choice(population, 40).mean() for _ in range(3000)])

print(f"population mean:        {population.mean():.3f}")
print(f"mean of sample means:  {sample_means.mean():.3f}")
print(f"SE (formula sigma/sqrt(n)): {population.std() / 40 ** 0.5:.3f}")
print(f"SE (simulated):             {sample_means.std():.3f}")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

The mean of the sample means lands on the population mean, and the two SE
figures nearly coincide, the CLT holding even though the raw data is far
from normal.

### What is the Law of Large Numbers, and how is it different from the CLT?

The Law of Large Numbers says the sample mean converges to the population
mean as $n$ grows, it tells you *where* the estimate ends up. The CLT tells
you *what shape* the estimate's uncertainty takes (normal) along the way.
Together they justify using large samples: the estimate homes in on the
truth, and its error is normally distributed.

### What is a confidence interval, really?

It's a procedure, not a probability about one interval. A 95% confidence
interval means: if you repeated the experiment many times and built an
interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true
parameter. For a mean it's

$$\bar{x} \pm z^{*}\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

with $z^{*} \approx 1.96$ for 95% (a t critical value for small samples).
It is **not** "a 95% probability the parameter is in *this* interval", the
parameter is fixed; the interval is what's random.

### What is bootstrapping, and when do you use it?

Bootstrapping estimates the sampling distribution of a statistic by
resampling your data **with replacement** many times and recomputing the
statistic each time. It needs no formula for the standard error, so it
works for medians, ratios, or any statistic where the analytic SE is hard
or unknown. The percentile method reads the confidence interval straight
off the resampled distribution:

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
data = rng.normal(loc=50, scale=10, size=200)

boot_means = np.array([
    rng.choice(data, size=len(data), replace=True).mean()   # resample w/ replacement
    for _ in range(5000)
])
low, high = np.percentile(boot_means, [2.5, 97.5])
print(f"sample mean:      {data.mean():.2f}")
print(f"95% bootstrap CI: ({low:.2f}, {high:.2f})")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

Each resample is the same size as the original with replacement, so some
rows repeat and others drop out; the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the
5,000 resampled means bracket the true mean with 95% coverage.

### What exactly is a p-value?

The probability of observing data **at least as extreme** as yours,
**assuming the null hypothesis is true**. A small p-value means your data
would be surprising under the null, so you reject it at your significance
level $\alpha$. It is **not** the probability the null is true, nor the
probability your result is a fluke, both are computed *given* the null.

### What is hypothesis testing, and what are its steps?

A formal procedure for deciding whether data provide enough evidence
against a null hypothesis $H_0$. State $H_0$ and the alternative $H_1$;
fix $\alpha$ and the sample size before collecting data; compute a test
statistic and its p-value; reject $H_0$ if $p < \alpha$. Fixing the
hypotheses and $\alpha$ in advance is what keeps the test honest.

### What is a permutation test?

A non-parametric test that builds the null distribution by shuffling group
labels: if the groups were truly exchangeable (no effect), reassigning
labels at random should produce differences as large as the observed one
often. The p-value is the fraction of shuffles whose difference is at least
as extreme. It assumes almost nothing about the distribution:

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
control   = rng.normal(100, 15, size=50)
treatment = rng.normal(107, 15, size=50)
observed  = treatment.mean() - control.mean()

pooled = np.concatenate([control, treatment])
n = len(control)
diffs = np.empty(10000)
for i in range(10000):
    perm = rng.permutation(pooled)
    diffs[i] = perm[:n].mean() - perm[n:].mean()

p_value = np.mean(np.abs(diffs) >= abs(observed))   # two-sided
print(f"observed difference: {observed:.2f}")
print(f"permutation p-value: {p_value:.4f}")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

### What are Type I and Type II errors, and what is power?

A **Type I error** is a false positive (rejecting a true null); its rate is
$\alpha$. A **Type II error** is a false negative (missing a real effect);
its rate is $\beta$, and **power** $= 1 - \beta$ is the chance of detecting
a true effect. Power rises with larger sample size, larger effect size,
lower variance, and higher $\alpha$, lowering $\alpha$ trades fewer false
positives for more false negatives.

### What sampling methods should you know?

**Simple random** gives every unit equal probability. **Stratified**
samples within subgroups to guarantee representation (and reduce variance).
**Cluster** samples whole groups to cut cost. **Reservoir** sampling draws
a uniform sample from a stream of unknown length in one pass. The method
shapes both bias and variance of every downstream estimate.

### What is selection (sampling) bias?

When the sample isn't representative of the population you want to
generalize to, so estimates are systematically off no matter how much data
you collect. Survivorship bias (only analyzing what survived) and
non-response bias are common forms. In ML it shows up as training on a
population that differs from serving traffic, which no amount of modeling
fixes.

### What is Bayes' theorem?

It updates a prior belief with evidence:

$$P(\theta \mid D) = \frac{P(D \mid \theta)\,P(\theta)}{P(D)}$$

It underpins spam filters, medical diagnosis, and Bayesian inference (prior
→ posterior after data). The classic trap: with a rare condition, even a
very accurate test yields a low posterior probability of being positive,
because base rates dominate.

### Covariance vs correlation, what's the difference?

Covariance measures whether two variables move together but its magnitude
depends on their units, so it's hard to interpret. Correlation
standardizes it to $[-1, 1]$:

$$\rho_{X,Y} = \frac{\text{Cov}(X, Y)}{\sigma_X \, \sigma_Y}$$

Correlation captures only **linear** association; a zero correlation can
still hide a strong non-linear relationship (e.g., $y = x^2$).

### Why does correlation not imply causation?

Two variables can correlate because one causes the other, because a third
**confounder** drives both, or by pure chance. Establishing causation needs
a randomized controlled experiment or a causal-inference method
(instrumental variables, difference-in-differences) that rules out
confounders. Ice-cream sales and drownings correlate, but temperature
causes both.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`A test returns \`p = 0.03\` at \`α = 0.05\`. Which statement is correct?

- There is a 3% probability the null hypothesis is true.
  > A p-value is not the probability the null is true; it's computed *assuming* the null holds.
- [o] If the null were true, there's a 3% chance of data this extreme or more, so at α = 0.05 you reject the null.
  > That's the definition; since 0.03 < 0.05, you reject.
- There's a 97% probability the effect is real and important.
  > Significance isn't the probability the effect is real, and says nothing about effect size.
- The result is guaranteed not to be a false positive.
  > A small p reduces but never eliminates the Type I error rate.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does a 95% confidence interval for a mean actually claim?

- There's a 95% probability the true mean lies in this specific interval.
  > The parameter is fixed, not random; probability describes the *procedure*, not this one interval.
- [o] If you repeated the experiment many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true mean.
  > That's the frequentist definition: coverage is a property of the long-run procedure.
- 95% of the data falls inside the interval.
  > A CI is about the *mean's* uncertainty, not the spread of the raw data.
- The interval is wrong 5% of the time for this dataset.
  > "This dataset" gives one interval that either contains the mean or not; 95% refers to repeated sampling.`}
/>

def pearson_correlation(x, y):
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)

    ym = y - y.mean()
