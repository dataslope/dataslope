## ML fundamentals

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What separates supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning?

- The size of the dataset each requires.
  > Data volume varies within each paradigm.
- [o] Supervised learns from labelled input-output pairs; unsupervised finds structure in unlabelled data; reinforcement learns a policy from rewards received by acting in an environment.
  > The distinguishing question is what feedback signal is available, not what algorithm is used.
- Whether a neural network is involved.
  > All three can use neural networks or not.
- Whether the output is continuous or discrete.
  > That distinguishes regression from classification within supervised learning.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does the bias–variance decomposition say about generalization error?

- Error is entirely due to insufficient data.
  > More data reduces variance, not bias.
- [o] Expected error splits into bias squared, variance, and irreducible noise; simple models carry high bias, flexible ones high variance, and the total is minimized somewhere between.
  > It explains why a badly underfit model does not improve with more data, and why an overfit one does.
- Bias and variance always move together.
  > They typically trade against each other as capacity changes.
- Variance can be eliminated with regularization.
  > Regularization reduces variance at the cost of bias; it does not remove it.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why split data into train, validation, and test rather than just train and test?

- To have more data to train on.
  > Splitting three ways leaves less for training.
- [o] The validation set is used repeatedly to tune hyperparameters, which leaks information into it; the test set stays untouched so the final estimate is honest.
  > Every decision made by looking at the test set spends a little of its credibility.
- Because cross-validation requires three sets.
  > Cross-validation replaces the validation split, using the folds instead.
- To detect data drift.
  > Drift is monitored in production.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is data leakage, and what is the structural defence?

- Training data being exposed publicly.
  > That is a security incident.
- [o] Information unavailable at prediction time entering training (a future-derived feature, or a transform fitted before the split), defended against by fitting every transform inside a pipeline on the training fold only.
  > The tell is validation performance that is too good and collapses in production.
- Overfitting to the training set.
  > Overfitting shows up in validation; leakage hides from it.
- Missing values imputed with the mean.
  > Mean imputation leaks only if the mean is computed over all the data, which is the pipeline point again.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When is k-fold cross-validation the wrong choice?

- When the dataset is large.
  > A large dataset makes it expensive, not wrong.
- [o] On time series, where random folds train on the future to predict the past; a rolling-origin or expanding-window split is the correct form.
  > Grouped data has the same problem: rows from one patient or one user must not straddle the split.
- When the model is a neural network.
  > Model family does not matter.
- When classes are imbalanced.
  > Stratified k-fold handles that.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is early stopping, and what is the subtlety?

- Halting training after a fixed number of epochs.
  > A fixed budget is not early stopping.
- [o] Stopping when validation performance stops improving, keeping the best checkpoint, but the epoch count is then itself a hyperparameter tuned on validation, so validation performance is optimistic.
  > It is a form of regularization, and it costs nothing extra to run.
- Stopping when training loss reaches zero.
  > Zero training loss is usually a sign of overfitting.
- Stopping when the learning rate reaches its minimum.
  > That is a scheduler ending.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do batch, stochastic, and mini-batch gradient descent differ?

- Only in the learning rate used.
  > They differ in how much data each update sees.
- [o] Batch uses the whole dataset per update (stable, slow); SGD uses one example (noisy, fast, can escape shallow minima); mini-batch uses a subset and is the practical default.
  > Mini-batch is also what makes GPU utilization efficient, which is the real reason it dominates.
- SGD is deterministic and batch is not.
  > The randomness is in SGD's sampling.
- Batch gradient descent cannot be parallelized.
  > It parallelizes well; it is simply expensive per step.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the F1 score, and when is it the wrong summary?

- The average of accuracy and recall.
  > It combines precision and recall.
- [o] The harmonic mean of precision and recall, misleading when the two errors have very different costs, since it weights them equally by construction.
  > When a false negative costs ten times a false positive, an Fβ score or an explicit cost analysis is more honest.
- The area under the ROC curve.
  > That is a different metric entirely.
- The accuracy on the positive class only.
  > That is recall.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What do RMSE, MAE, and R² each tell you?

- They are interchangeable regression metrics.
  > They respond differently to outliers and to scale.
- [o] RMSE penalizes large errors quadratically and stays in the target's units; MAE weights all errors linearly and is more robust; R² reports the fraction of variance explained, and is scale-free.
  > A large gap between RMSE and MAE is itself a signal that a few large errors dominate.
- RMSE is for classification and MAE for regression.
  > All three are regression metrics.
- R² is always between 0 and 1.
  > On unseen data a model worse than predicting the mean gives a negative R².`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a parametric versus a non-parametric model?

- Parametric models have hyperparameters; non-parametric ones do not.
  > Both have hyperparameters.
- [o] A parametric model has a fixed number of parameters regardless of data size; a non-parametric model's complexity grows with the data; kNN keeps every training point.
  > It is why kNN's inference cost scales with the training set while linear regression's does not.
- Non-parametric models make no assumptions at all.
  > They make weaker distributional assumptions, not none.
- Parametric models cannot overfit.
  > They can, particularly with many parameters relative to data.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does kNN make a prediction, and what is its main practical cost?

- It fits a local linear model at each query point.
  > That is locally weighted regression.
- [o] It finds the k nearest training points by a distance metric and votes or averages their targets; the cost is that inference scans the training set and the distance metric degrades in high dimensions.
  > Feature scaling is mandatory, since an unscaled large-magnitude feature dominates the distance.
- It builds a tree and traverses it.
  > Index structures such as KD-trees accelerate the search; they are not the algorithm.
- It learns weights by gradient descent.
  > There is no training phase beyond storing the data.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does a support vector machine classify?

- By fitting a probability distribution to each class.
  > That is a generative classifier.
- [o] By finding the hyperplane that maximizes the margin between classes, using only the support vectors, with a kernel to handle non-linear boundaries implicitly.
  > The kernel trick computes inner products in a high-dimensional space without ever constructing it, which is what makes it tractable.
- By recursively splitting the feature space.
  > That is a decision tree.
- By averaging the nearest labelled examples.
  > That is kNN.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are embeddings, in the general ML sense?

- Compressed copies of the raw input.
  > Compression is incidental; the geometry is the point.
- [o] Dense, learned vector representations where distance encodes similarity, letting discrete items (words, users, products) be used by models that need continuous input.
  > It is also why one model's embeddings can power retrieval, clustering, and recommendation without retraining.
- The final layer of a neural network.
  > That is usually the output head.
- One-hot encoded categorical features.
  > One-hot vectors are sparse and carry no similarity structure.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the curse of dimensionality?

- Models take longer to train with more features.
  > Compute cost is real but not the phenomenon.
- [o] As dimensions grow, the volume of the space grows so fast that data becomes sparse and distances between points converge, undermining distance-based methods.
  > It is why kNN and clustering degrade in high dimensions and why dimensionality reduction helps them specifically.
- High-dimensional data cannot be visualized.
  > A practical inconvenience, not the statistical problem.
- More features always cause overfitting.
  > Overfitting risk rises but is not guaranteed.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does gradient boosting do that a Random Forest does not?

- Sample features at each split.
  > That is a Random Forest's distinguishing move.
- [o] Fit each new tree to the residual errors of the ensemble so far, reducing bias sequentially rather than averaging independent trees to reduce variance.
  > It typically achieves higher accuracy and is much easier to overfit, so learning rate, depth, and early stopping carry real weight.
- Train trees in parallel.
  > Boosting is inherently sequential.
- Use only shallow trees.
  > Shallow trees are conventional in boosting but not the defining difference.`}
/>

## Deep learning

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does a single artificial neuron compute?

- A weighted sum of its inputs.
  > That is only the first half.
- [o] A weighted sum plus a bias, passed through a non-linear activation.
  > Without the activation the whole network collapses to one linear map, which is why the non-linearity is essential rather than optional.
- The dot product of two input vectors.
  > It weights one input vector by learned parameters.
- A probability between 0 and 1.
  > Only with a sigmoid activation; ReLU is unbounded above.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Compare sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU.

- They are interchangeable in practice.
  > Their gradient behavior differs sharply.
- [o] Sigmoid squashes to (0,1) and saturates, tanh is zero-centred but also saturates, ReLU is cheap and non-saturating for positive inputs but can produce dead units.
  > Leaky ReLU and GELU exist to keep ReLU's benefits while avoiding units that output zero forever.
- ReLU is bounded and sigmoid is not.
  > It is the other way round.
- Only sigmoid can be used in hidden layers.
  > ReLU is the default in hidden layers.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why are CNNs well suited to images compared with a fully-connected network?

- They have more parameters, so they capture more detail.
  > They have far fewer, which is part of the advantage.
- [o] Local receptive fields and weight sharing encode translation equivariance and cut parameters enormously, which is a strong and correct prior for images.
  > A fully-connected layer on a 224×224 image would need tens of millions of parameters in the first layer alone.
- They process pixels sequentially.
  > Convolutions apply in parallel across the image.
- They do not require activation functions.
  > They need them exactly as much as any network.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What do LSTMs and GRUs add over a vanilla RNN?

- More layers.
  > Depth is orthogonal.
- [o] Gating that controls what to keep, forget, and output, providing a path along which gradients flow without repeated multiplicative shrinkage.
  > GRUs merge some of the LSTM's gates for fewer parameters and often comparable performance.
- Attention over the full sequence.
  > Attention is a separate mechanism, added later.
- Bidirectional processing.
  > Any RNN can be made bidirectional.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Describe the transformer architecture at a high level.

- Stacked convolutions with residual connections.
  > Convolutions are not the core operation.
- [o] Stacked blocks of multi-head self-attention plus a position-wise feed-forward network, each wrapped in residual connections and layer normalization, with positional information injected since attention is order-agnostic.
  > Removing recurrence is what allows the whole sequence to be processed in parallel during training.
- A recurrent encoder feeding a recurrent decoder.
  > That is the pre-transformer seq2seq design.
- An encoder-only network with no feed-forward layers.
  > The feed-forward sublayer holds a large share of the parameters.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does layer normalization differ from batch normalization?

- Layer norm normalizes across the batch.
  > That is batch norm.
- [o] Layer norm normalizes across the features of each individual sample, so it is independent of batch size and works with sequences of varying length.
  > That independence is why transformers use it; batch statistics are awkward with variable-length sequences and small batches.
- They are equivalent for fully-connected layers.
  > They normalize along different axes and give different results.
- Layer norm is applied only at inference.
  > It is active in both phases, unlike batch norm's running statistics.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What causes exploding gradients, and what is the standard remedy?

- Too small a learning rate.
  > That causes slow convergence.
- [o] Repeated multiplication by values greater than one through depth or time steps, remedied by gradient clipping, better initialization, and normalization layers.
  > Clipping by global norm is the routine defence in RNN and transformer training.
- Dropout applied too aggressively.
  > Dropout does not cause explosion.
- Using ReLU activations.
  > ReLU mitigates vanishing gradients; it is not a primary cause of explosion.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is transfer learning, and when is full fine-tuning worth it over feature extraction?

- They are the same technique.
  > They differ in which parameters are updated.
- [o] Transfer learning reuses a pretrained model; feature extraction freezes the backbone and trains a new head, while full fine-tuning updates everything, worth it when you have enough task data and the domain differs from pretraining.
  > With a small dataset, full fine-tuning tends to overfit and destroy the pretrained representations.
- Fine-tuning is always better.
  > On small datasets it frequently is not.
- Feature extraction requires retraining from scratch.
  > It trains only the new head.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is cross-entropy paired with softmax for classification?

- Softmax requires it mathematically.
  > Other losses are computable; they are just worse.
- [o] Their composition yields a clean gradient (predicted probability minus target), which avoids the vanishing gradient MSE suffers on a saturated output.
  > It is also why frameworks fuse the two into one numerically-stable operation rather than computing them separately.
- Cross-entropy is faster to compute.
  > Speed is not the reason.
- Softmax outputs are not probabilities without it.
  > Softmax outputs a distribution regardless of the loss.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why subtract the maximum before exponentiating in a softmax?

- To make the outputs sum to one.
  > Division by the sum does that; the shift is separate.
- [o] For numerical stability: exponentiating a large logit overflows, and subtracting the max leaves the result mathematically identical while keeping every exponent at most zero.
  > It is the standard trick, and it is why you should use the framework's fused implementation rather than writing it by hand.
- To centre the distribution at zero.
  > Centring is not the goal.
- To speed up the exponential.
  > Any speed difference is negligible.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does a "dead ReLU" mean, and what causes it?

- A neuron whose weights are exactly zero.
  > Zero weights are unusual and not what the term describes.
- [o] A unit whose pre-activation is negative for every input, so it outputs zero and receives no gradient, permanently, usually caused by too large a learning rate or a bad initialization.
  > Leaky ReLU, ELU, and GELU all keep a small gradient on the negative side to avoid it.
- A neuron removed by dropout.
  > Dropout is temporary and random.
- A neuron that always outputs its maximum value.
  > ReLU is unbounded above, so there is no maximum to saturate at.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How should the learning rate be scheduled in practice?

- Fixed for the whole run.
  > A fixed rate is rarely optimal at both the start and the end.
- [o] Usually a short warmup followed by decay (cosine or step), so early updates are stable and later ones are fine-grained.
  > Warmup matters most with adaptive optimizers and large batches, where the early moment estimates are unreliable.
- Increased steadily throughout training.
  > A growing step size destabilizes convergence.
- Set to the largest value that does not produce NaN.
  > That is the divergence boundary, not a good operating point.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What do residual (skip) connections contribute?

- They reduce the parameter count.
  > Parameters are unchanged.
- [o] They give gradients a direct path around each block, so very deep networks stay trainable and each block only has to learn a correction.
  > It is the change that took networks from tens of layers to hundreds, and it appears in every transformer block.
- They replace the need for normalization.
  > Residuals and normalization are used together.
- They allow layers to be skipped at inference for speed.
  > The connection is always active.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Compare Xavier (Glorot) and He initialization.

- They are the same scheme under two names.
  > They scale differently.
- [o] Xavier targets a variance suited to symmetric activations such as tanh; He scales up to account for ReLU zeroing out roughly half the units.
  > Using Xavier with deep ReLU networks makes activations shrink layer by layer.
- He initialization sets all weights to a constant.
  > Both draw from a distribution.
- Xavier applies only to convolutional layers.
  > Both apply to any weight matrix.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does batch size interact with the learning rate?

- Larger batches require a smaller learning rate.
  > The relationship usually runs the other way.
- [o] A larger batch gives a lower-variance gradient estimate, so a proportionally larger learning rate is typically usable: the linear scaling rule, usually with warmup.
  > Very large batches can still generalize worse, which is why the scaling is a heuristic rather than a law.
- They are entirely independent hyperparameters.
  > They are among the most coupled pair in training.
- Batch size only affects memory.
  > Memory is the obvious constraint and not the only effect.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does gradient accumulation achieve?

- It speeds up training on a single GPU.
  > It is slightly slower per effective batch.
- [o] It simulates a large batch on limited memory by summing gradients over several forward/backward passes before stepping the optimizer.
  > The subtlety is that batch-norm statistics still use the small physical batch, so results are not identical to a true large batch.
- It stores gradients for later analysis.
  > Nothing is retained after the step.
- It replaces the need for an optimizer.
  > The optimizer still applies the accumulated gradient.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is knowledge distillation?

- Removing weights below a threshold.
  > That is pruning.
- [o] Training a smaller student model to match a larger teacher's outputs, typically its soft probability distribution, which carries more information than hard labels.
  > The temperature-softened logits are what convey the teacher's relative confidence across classes.
- Reducing numeric precision.
  > That is quantization.
- Training on a subset of the data.
  > Sampling is unrelated.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is data augmentation effective, and where does it go wrong?

- It increases the true information in the dataset.
  > No new information is created; the invariances are what is being taught.
- [o] It teaches invariances by presenting label-preserving variations, reducing overfitting, and it goes wrong when a transform changes the label, such as flipping an image of a digit.
  > Applying augmentation to the validation set is the other common error, since it makes the measurement inconsistent.
- It always improves accuracy.
  > A badly chosen transform can hurt.
- It replaces the need for regularization.
  > It is one form of regularization among several.`}
/>

## NLP & LLMs

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is subword tokenization the standard?

- It produces shorter sequences than word tokenization.
  > Sequences are usually longer than word-level ones.
- [o] It bounds the vocabulary while still representing any string, so rare and unseen words decompose into known pieces instead of becoming a single unknown token.
  > BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece are the common algorithms, and they also handle morphology and code reasonably.
- It removes the need for embeddings.
  > Tokens are still embedded.
- It guarantees each token is a morpheme.
  > The splits are learned from frequency, not linguistics.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What distinguishes contextual from static embeddings?

- Contextual embeddings are larger.
  > Dimensionality is a design choice.
- [o] A static embedding gives one vector per word regardless of usage; a contextual embedding depends on the surrounding sentence, so "bank" differs between a river and a loan.
  > word2vec and GloVe are static; the representations inside BERT or a GPT-style model are contextual.
- Static embeddings are trained on more data.
  > Data volume is not the distinction.
- Contextual embeddings do not need pretraining.
  > They come specifically from pretrained models.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why did transformers replace RNNs for most NLP?

- They use fewer parameters.
  > They typically use far more.
- [o] Removing recurrence lets the whole sequence be processed in parallel during training, and attention gives every position direct access to every other, so long dependencies do not have to survive many sequential steps.
  > The parallelism is what made training at internet scale practical.
- They do not require large datasets.
  > They are more data-hungry, not less.
- They avoid the need for positional information.
  > They need it added explicitly, precisely because attention is order-agnostic.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and what does LoRA do?

- Training only the final layer.
  > That is classic feature extraction, one narrow form of the idea.
- [o] Adapting a large model by training a small number of extra or modified parameters; LoRA injects trainable low-rank matrices alongside frozen weights, and they can be merged at inference.
  > It cuts memory and checkpoint size enormously, and lets many task-specific adapters share one base model.
- Quantizing the model to fewer bits.
  > That is quantization, a serving optimization.
- Distilling into a smaller model.
  > Distillation trains a separate student model.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When would you choose RAG over fine-tuning?

- When you need the model to adopt a specific output format.
  > Format and style are what fine-tuning does well.
- [o] When the model needs current, frequently-changing, or source-attributable knowledge, since the retrieval corpus can be updated without touching the weights.
  > The two compose: fine-tune for behavior and format, retrieve for facts.
- When latency is critical.
  > RAG adds a retrieval hop, so it usually costs latency.
- When the training data is small.
  > Small data does favour RAG, but currency and attribution are the decisive reasons.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What causes hallucination, and what mitigates it?

- Insufficient model parameters; scale up.
  > Larger models hallucinate more fluently, not less reliably.
- [o] The model is trained to produce plausible continuations, not to check truth, so it fills gaps confidently; grounding with retrieval, requiring citations, and constrained decoding all reduce it.
  > None of these eliminate it, which is why high-stakes outputs need verification rather than trust.
- A temperature setting of zero.
  > Greedy decoding reduces randomness without guaranteeing factuality.
- Tokenization errors.
  > Tokenization is not the source.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do temperature and top-p control generation?

- Temperature sets the maximum output length.
  > Length is controlled separately.
- [o] Temperature scales the logits before sampling: lower is more deterministic; top-p samples from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds p, adapting the candidate pool per step.
  > Setting temperature to 0 makes decoding greedy, which is usually what you want for extraction and classification.
- Top-p limits the vocabulary to the p most common tokens.
  > That is top-k; top-p is defined by cumulative probability.
- They are two names for the same parameter.
  > They interact but are distinct.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is attention's cost a bottleneck for long documents?

- Memory grows linearly with sequence length.
  > Linear growth would not be a bottleneck.
- [o] Every position attends to every other, so time and memory scale quadratically with sequence length.
  > FlashAttention improves the constant and memory pattern; sparse, linear, and sliding-window attention change the asymptotics.
- Positional encodings become ambiguous.
  > Extrapolation is a real issue, but not the cost bottleneck.
- The vocabulary grows with document length.
  > Vocabulary is fixed.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you evaluate an LLM-based feature meaningfully?

- Perplexity on a held-out corpus.
  > Perplexity measures language modelling, not task usefulness.
- [o] A task-specific evaluation set with graded outputs, combining automatic metrics, model-based grading, and human review, plus checks for regressions on previously-fixed failures.
  > A fixed regression suite is what stops a prompt change from quietly breaking last month's fix.
- User satisfaction surveys alone.
  > Useful signal, far too slow and coarse to iterate on.
- BLEU score in all cases.
  > BLEU suits translation and correlates poorly with quality on open-ended generation.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a context window, and why does its size matter?

- The number of documents retrieved in RAG.
  > Retrieval count is a separate parameter.
- [o] The maximum number of tokens the model can attend to at once, covering prompt plus generation; it bounds how much can be supplied in-context and interacts directly with attention's quadratic cost.
  > Models also tend to use the middle of a long context less effectively, so a bigger window is not automatically better use of it.
- The size of the model's vocabulary.
  > Vocabulary is unrelated.
- The number of layers in the transformer.
  > Depth is a separate dimension.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What objectives are used to pretrain language models?

- Supervised classification on labelled corpora.
  > Pretraining is self-supervised precisely to avoid needing labels.
- [o] Self-supervised objectives derived from the text itself: next-token prediction for autoregressive models, masked-token prediction for encoder models.
  > It is what allows training on internet-scale text with no annotation.
- Reinforcement learning from human feedback.
  > RLHF is an alignment stage applied after pretraining.
- Contrastive learning between sentence pairs.
  > Used for embedding models, not general pretraining.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting?

- Three names for the same technique.
  > They differ in what the prompt supplies.
- [o] Zero-shot gives the instruction alone; few-shot adds worked examples in the prompt; chain-of-thought asks for intermediate reasoning before the answer.
  > Chain-of-thought helps most on multi-step problems and costs more tokens, which is a real latency and price consideration.
- Three sizes of training dataset.
  > None of them involve training.
- Three decoding strategies.
  > Decoding is controlled separately by temperature and top-p.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are the moving parts of a RAG system?

- A single model with a longer context window.
  > A long context is an alternative to RAG, not its implementation.
- [o] Chunking and embedding a corpus, an index for similarity search, a retriever fetching relevant chunks at query time, and a generator conditioned on them.
  > Chunk size and overlap, plus a reranker, usually matter more to output quality than the choice of generator.
- A fine-tuned model plus a cache.
  > Caching is an optimization, not a component of retrieval.
- A vector database only.
  > The store is one piece of the pipeline.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are embeddings used for beyond feeding a model?

- Only as model input.
  > Their reuse is much of their value.
- [o] Semantic search and retrieval, clustering and deduplication, recommendation, and similarity-based classification, all by comparing vectors rather than running the model again.
  > It is why an embedding endpoint is often the most-used part of an LLM stack.
- Compressing text for storage.
  > Embeddings are lossy and not invertible, so they cannot serve as compression.
- Tokenizing input.
  > Tokenization happens before embedding.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do word2vec and GloVe differ?

- word2vec is contextual and GloVe is static.
  > Both produce static embeddings.
- [o] word2vec learns predictively from local context windows (skip-gram or CBOW); GloVe factorizes a global word co-occurrence matrix.
  > They land in a similar place, which is part of why both were superseded by contextual models rather than by each other.
- GloVe requires labelled data.
  > Both are unsupervised.
- word2vec produces one vector per sentence.
  > It produces one per word.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does chunk size matter in a retrieval pipeline?

- It determines the embedding dimension.
  > Dimension is fixed by the model.
- [o] Chunks too small lose the context needed to answer; too large dilute the embedding with unrelated content and waste context window on irrelevant text.
  > Overlapping chunks and retrieving neighbours are the usual ways to keep boundaries from splitting an answer in half.
- It controls retrieval latency only.
  > Latency changes, but relevance is the bigger effect.
- Smaller chunks are always better for precision.

## ML system design

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When do you choose batch inference over online inference?

- Whenever the model is large.
  > Model size affects cost, not the fundamental choice.
- [o] When predictions can be computed ahead of time for a known set of entities and served as lookups: daily churn scores, precomputed recommendations.
  > It is dramatically simpler and cheaper; online inference is for when the input is only known at request time.
- When the input arrives as a stream.
  > Streaming input usually argues for online or near-online scoring.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What problem does a feature store solve?

- Storing trained model artifacts.
  > That is a model registry.
- [o] It gives training and serving one definition of each feature, with an offline store for historical point-in-time-correct training data and an online store for low-latency lookups.
  > Without it, the same feature gets implemented twice in two languages and quietly diverges, which is training–serving skew.
- Caching model predictions.
  > Prediction caching is separate.
- Versioning the training dataset.
  > Data versioning is adjacent but distinct.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is training–serving skew?

- The model performing worse over time.
  > That is drift or degradation.
- [o] A systematic difference between the features seen in training and those computed at serving time: different code paths, different aggregation windows, or a leaked future value.
  > It shows up as offline metrics that do not survive contact with production, and it is one of the most common causes of a "good" model failing.
- The difference between train and test accuracy.
  > That is overfitting.
- Latency differences between environments.
  > Latency is an operational concern.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do data drift, concept drift, and performance degradation differ?

- They are three names for the same thing.
  > They have different causes and different responses.
- [o] Data drift is a change in the input distribution; concept drift is a change in the input-to-output relationship; performance degradation is the observable drop in metrics, which either can cause.
  > Data drift is detectable without labels; concept drift usually is not, which is why label delay matters so much.
- Concept drift only affects classification.
  > It affects any supervised task.
- Data drift always causes performance degradation.
  > A model can be robust to a shift in a feature it barely uses.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you detect drift when labels arrive weeks late or never?

- Wait for the labels.
  > That leaves the model unmonitored for the whole delay.
- [o] Monitor unlabelled proxies (input feature distributions, prediction distribution, and the model's confidence) against a training baseline, and alert on divergence.
  > PSI and KL divergence are the usual measures; a shifting prediction distribution is often the earliest signal available.
- Retrain on a schedule and assume it is fine.
  > Blind retraining can bake in a problem rather than fix it.
- Compare against a second model's predictions.
  > A useful check, but both can drift together.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a shadow deployment?

  > That is a canary.
- [o] Running the new model on real traffic in parallel with the current one, logging its predictions without serving them, to compare behavior and latency at zero user risk.
  > It validates the serving path and the prediction distribution; it cannot measure business impact, since nobody sees the output.

  > Staging usually lacks real traffic.
- Running the model on historical data.
  > That is backtesting.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does a canary deployment differ from shadow testing?

- Canary uses synthetic traffic.
  > It uses real traffic and real users.
- [o] A canary actually serves a small share of users, so business metrics can be measured and harm is bounded; shadow serves nobody and measures only technical behavior.

  > It typically shares infrastructure with weighted routing.

  > Shadow predictions are never shown, so no behavioral metric exists.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What belongs in a model registry?

- Only the serialized model weights.
  > Weights alone cannot be traced or reproduced.
- [o] Versioned models with their lineage (training data and code versions, hyperparameters, evaluation metrics), plus stage transitions and approval history.

  > Data is versioned separately and referenced by the registry.
- Live prediction logs.
  > Those go to monitoring.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What triggers retraining in a well-run system?

- [o] A combination: a schedule as a baseline, plus drift and performance thresholds, plus events such as a new data source, with an evaluation gate before anything is promoted.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is an evaluation gate in a training pipeline?

  > That is CI for the code.
- [o] An automated check comparing the candidate model against the incumbent on a held-out set and on key segments, blocking promotion unless it clears the bar.

  > Latency belongs in the gate but is not the whole of it.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does request batching improve GPU throughput?

- [o] It amortizes kernel launch overhead and keeps the GPU's parallel units saturated, so throughput rises, at the cost of added per-request latency while the batch fills.

  > It requires a queue.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does model quantization do, and what does it cost?

  > That is pruning.
- [o] Represents weights and sometimes activations at lower precision (int8 or 4-bit instead of fp32), cutting memory and often latency, at some accuracy cost.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How would you handle cold start in a recommender?

- [o] Fall back to content-based features and popularity for new users or items, and collect signal quickly through onboarding preferences or light exploration.

  > A poor first experience means they may never accumulate.
- Train a separate model per user.
  > There is no data to train it on.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Sketch the core of a fraud detection system. What is the defining constraint?

- Maximizing accuracy.
  > At a fraction of a percent fraud rate, accuracy is meaningless.
- [o] Extreme class imbalance and highly asymmetric costs, with a hard latency budget and an adversary who adapts, so precision-recall trade-offs, fast features, and frequent retraining dominate the design.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the population stability index used for?

- [o] Quantifying how much a feature's or score's distribution has shifted from a baseline, as a label-free drift signal.

## MLOps

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does MLOps differ from ordinary DevOps?

- [o] Behavior depends on data as well as code, so data and models must be versioned and tested too, and a system can degrade with no code change at all.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What do you need to version to reproduce a model?

  > Weights alone cannot be re-derived.
- [o] Code, training data (or a snapshot reference), the environment and dependency versions, hyperparameters, and the random seeds.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is continuous training?

- [o] An automated pipeline that retrains on fresh data when triggered by schedule, drift, or new data, with evaluation gates before any promotion.

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a champion/challenger setup?

- [o] The incumbent serves traffic while one or more candidates are evaluated in parallel (shadow or on a small share), and promoted only when they demonstrably beat it.

- Training two models and averaging them.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What should you monitor for a model in production?

- [o] Operational health (latency, error rate, throughput), data health (schema, nulls, distributions), model behavior (prediction distribution, confidence), and business outcomes where labels allow.

- Only the input feature distributions.

- Only the business KPI.

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

- It speeds up training.

- [o] Training on silently corrupted input produces a model that is confidently wrong, and the failure surfaces much later and much more expensively than a failed check would have.

<MultipleChoice

- [o] Latency is the time for one request; throughput is requests served per unit time, and batching raises throughput while adding latency.

  > It is central to sizing an online service too.

<MultipleChoice

- [o] For large models, especially transformers, where the arithmetic is parallel enough and traffic high enough to keep the device busy after batching.

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

## Statistics, probability & coding for ML

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the difference between a PMF and a PDF?

- A PMF applies to continuous variables.
  > It applies to discrete ones.
- [o] A PMF gives the probability of each discrete outcome; a PDF gives a density for a continuous variable, where only the area under a region is a probability.
  > It is why a PDF's value can exceed 1 while a PMF's cannot.
- A PDF sums to one across its outcomes.
  > It integrates to one; summation is the discrete case.
- They are the same function under different names.
  > They are defined for different variable types.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is bootstrapping, and when is it useful?

- Training a model on its own predictions.
  > That is self-training.
- [o] Resampling the observed data with replacement to approximate the sampling distribution of a statistic, useful when no closed-form standard error exists.
  > Confidence intervals for a median, an AUC, or any custom metric are the typical uses.
- Initializing weights before training.
  > Unrelated to initialization.
- Removing outliers iteratively.
  > That is trimming.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a permutation test?

- A test of whether data is normally distributed.
  > That is a normality test.
- [o] Repeatedly shuffling the group labels to build the distribution of the statistic under the null of no difference, then locating the observed value in it.
  > It assumes very little about the distribution, which makes it a good default for an unusual statistic or a small sample.
- A test that permutes features to measure importance.
  > Permutation *importance* borrows the idea for a different purpose.
- A non-parametric alternative to bootstrapping.
  > They are related resampling methods answering different questions.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does NumPy vectorization beat a Python \`for\` loop?

- NumPy uses multiple CPU cores by default.
  > Most element-wise operations are single-threaded.
- [o] The loop runs in compiled C over a contiguous, typed array, avoiding per-element Python object overhead and interpreter dispatch.
  > Contiguity also lets the CPU prefetch and vectorize, which is where the remaining factor comes from.
- It uses less memory.
  > Arrays are more compact, but speed comes from the execution model.
- It automatically uses the GPU.
  > NumPy is CPU-only; that is what CuPy and framework tensors are for.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is broadcasting in NumPy?

- Copying an array to every worker process.
  > That is a distributed-computing broadcast.
- [o] Automatically expanding dimensions of size one so arrays of different shapes can be combined element-wise, without materializing the expansion.
  > Subtracting a per-column mean from a matrix is the everyday case: shape \`(n, d)\` against \`(d,)\`.
- Converting an array to a different dtype.
  > That is casting.
- Flattening a multi-dimensional array.
  > That is reshaping.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you find the top-k scores efficiently from n items when k is small?

- Sort the whole array and take the first k.
  > O(n log n) when you do not need the rest ordered.
- [o] \`np.argpartition\` in O(n), then sort only the k selected, or a size-k heap when streaming.
  > The distinction matters when n is millions and k is ten.
- Loop and track the maximum k times.
  > O(nk), fine for tiny k and worse as k grows.
- Use a set to deduplicate first.
  > Deduplication is a different operation.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you compute cosine similarity between two vectors in NumPy?

- \`np.dot(a, b)\`
  > That is the unnormalized dot product.
- [o] \`np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))\`
  > Pre-normalizing every vector once turns a batch of similarities into a single matrix multiplication, which is how vector search works.
- \`np.corrcoef(a, b)[0, 1]\`
  > Correlation centres both vectors first, which is a different measure.
- \`1 / np.linalg.norm(a - b)\`
  > That is a function of Euclidean distance.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why do float32 and float64 matter for ML data?

- float64 always gives better model accuracy.
  > Model accuracy rarely improves, while memory doubles.
- [o] float32 halves memory and bandwidth and is what GPUs are optimized for, which is why frameworks default to it, with float16 or bfloat16 going further at some numerical risk.
  > Accidentally holding a large dataset as float64 is a common and needless doubling of memory.
- float32 cannot represent negative numbers.
  > It represents the full signed range.
- The difference only affects training, not inference.
  > It affects both.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you shuffle features and labels together correctly?

- Call a shuffle on each array separately.
  > That destroys the correspondence between rows and labels.
- [o] Generate one permutation of the indices and apply it to both arrays.
  > \`sklearn.utils.shuffle\` does exactly this, and setting a seed makes the split reproducible.
- Sort both arrays by a random column.
  > A workable hack that is easy to get subtly wrong.
- Shuffle only the features, since labels are aligned by position.
  > Alignment by position is precisely what shuffling one side breaks.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is reservoir sampling, and when do you need it?

- Sampling with replacement from a fixed dataset.
  > That is bootstrap sampling.
- [o] Maintaining a uniform random sample of k items from a stream of unknown length in one pass, replacing an existing item with probability k/i at the i-th element.
  > It is the answer when the data does not fit in memory and you cannot count it in advance.
- Sampling proportionally to a weight.
  > That is weighted sampling, a related variant.
- Buffering a stream before sampling.
  > Buffering everything is exactly what it avoids.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does the Central Limit Theorem let an ML engineer do?

- Assume any dataset is normally distributed.
  > The data itself need not be.
- [o] Treat the sampling distribution of a mean (a mean loss, a mean metric across batches) as approximately normal, which underpins confidence intervals on evaluation results.
  > It is why a metric measured on a reasonable test set comes with a computable margin of error.
- Justify using MSE as a loss.
  > MSE follows from a Gaussian noise assumption on the residuals, not from the CLT.
- Ignore outliers.
  > Outliers still affect means, and heavy tails slow convergence to normality.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is selection bias, and how does it reach a training set?

- Choosing the wrong features.
  > That is feature selection.
- [o] The training sample is not representative of the population the model will serve: a fraud model trained only on reviewed transactions, or a survey answered only by active users.
  > The model then performs well offline and badly on the population it never saw.
- Sampling with replacement.
  > That is bootstrapping.
- Selecting the best model on the test set.
  > That is test-set overfitting, a related but different error.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does correlation not imply causation, and what would establish it?

- Correlation is unreliable on small samples.
  > Sample size affects precision, not the logical gap.
- [o] A confounder, reverse causation, or selection can all produce association without a causal link; randomized assignment, or a credible quasi-experimental design, is what closes the gap.
  > For a model this often does not matter, until someone proposes intervening on a feature, at which point it matters entirely.
- It does when the correlation exceeds 0.9.
  > Strength does not convert association into causation.
- Only experiments produce correlations.
  > Observational data produces them routinely.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you compute pairwise Euclidean distances between the rows of a matrix efficiently?

- A nested Python loop over row pairs.
  > It produces the right answer, hundreds of times slower than the vectorized form.
- [o] Expand the square: use broadcasting or \`scipy.spatial.distance.cdist\`, which compute all pairs in compiled code with one matrix multiplication.
  > The expansion trick can produce small negative values from floating-point error, so clip at zero before taking the square root.
- Sort the rows first, then compare neighbours.
  > Sorting does not give all pairwise distances.
- Use \`np.linalg.norm\` on the whole matrix.
  > That returns a single norm, not a distance matrix.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the complexity of a naive kNN prediction, and how does a heap help?

- O(1), since it just looks up neighbours.
  > Every training point has to be considered.
- [o] O(n log n) if you sort all distances; keeping a size-k heap brings it to O(n log k), and a spatial index or approximate search does better still in low dimensions.
  > At high dimensionality exact indexes lose their advantage, which is why approximate nearest neighbour libraries dominate vector search.
- O(k), independent of the training set size.
  > Cost scales with n.
- O(n²) for a single prediction.
  > That is the cost of all-pairs distances, not one query.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you compute precision and recall from scratch given predictions and labels?

- Precision is correct predictions over total predictions.
  > That is accuracy.
- [o] Precision is TP / (TP + FP); recall is TP / (TP + FN). One measures how many flagged items were right, the other how many real positives were caught.
  > Guard the denominators: a model that predicts no positives has an undefined precision rather than a zero one.
- Precision is TP / (TP + FN).
  > That is recall.
- Recall is TN / (TN + FP).
  > That is specificity.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you decode class predictions from a probability matrix?

- Take the row-wise sum.
  > Each row already sums to one under softmax.
- [o] \`np.argmax(probs, axis=1)\` for the predicted class index, or threshold a chosen class when the decision cost is asymmetric.
  > Taking argmax implicitly assumes equal error costs, which is a decision worth making explicitly.
- Round each probability to 0 or 1.
  > That can produce several positives or none in a single-label setting.
- \`np.max(probs, axis=1)\`
  > That returns the confidence, not which class it belongs to.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you implement mini-batching for training?

- Slice the array into fixed chunks in order, every epoch.
  > A fixed order lets the model learn the ordering, particularly if the data is sorted by label.
- [o] Shuffle the indices each epoch, then yield consecutive slices of the permutation, deciding deliberately whether to drop or keep the final partial batch.
  > Keeping the ragged last batch is fine unless batch norm is involved, where a batch of one causes trouble.
- Sample rows randomly with replacement each step.
  > That is bootstrap sampling; some rows are seen twice per epoch and others not at all.
- Load the whole dataset and pass it in one batch.
  > That is full-batch gradient descent.`}
