## ML fundamentals

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What separates supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning?

- The size of the labelled dataset each one requires.
  > Data volume varies within each paradigm.
- [o] The feedback signal available: labels, none, or rewards.
  > Supervised learns from labelled input-output pairs, unsupervised finds structure in unlabelled data, and reinforcement learns a policy from rewards earned by acting. What separates them is the feedback available, not the algorithm used.
- Whether a neural network or a classical algorithm is involved.
  > All three can use neural networks or not.
- Whether the output variable is continuous or discrete.
  > That distinguishes regression from classification within supervised learning.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does the bias–variance decomposition say about generalization error?

- Generalization error comes down entirely to having too little data.
  > More data reduces variance, not bias.
- [o] It splits into bias squared, variance, and irreducible noise.
  > Simple models carry high bias and low variance, flexible ones the reverse, and total error is minimized somewhere between. It explains why an underfit model does not improve with more data and an overfit one does.
- Bias and variance always rise and fall together.
  > They typically trade against each other as capacity changes.
- Regularization can eliminate variance entirely.
  > Regularization reduces variance at the cost of bias; it does not remove it.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why split data into train, validation, and test rather than just train and test?

- It leaves more data available for training.
  > Splitting three ways leaves less for training.
- [o] Repeated tuning leaks information into the validation set.
  > The test set stays untouched so the final estimate is honest. Every decision made by looking at the test set spends a little of its credibility.
- Because k-fold cross-validation requires exactly three splits.
  > Cross-validation replaces the validation split, using the folds instead.
- To detect data drift before deployment.
  > Drift is monitored in production, against live traffic.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is data leakage, and what is the structural defence?

- Training data being accidentally exposed to the public internet.
  > That is a security incident.
- [o] Information unavailable at prediction time entering training.
  > A future-derived feature or a transform fitted before the split will both do it. The defence is a pipeline that fits every transform inside the training fold. The tell is validation performance that is too good and collapses in production.
- Overfitting the model to the training set.
  > Overfitting shows up in validation; leakage hides from it.
- Imputing missing values with the column mean.
  > Mean imputation leaks only if the mean is computed over all the data, which is the pipeline point again.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When is k-fold cross-validation the wrong choice?

- When the dataset is very large.
  > A large dataset makes it expensive, not wrong.
- [o] On time series, where random folds leak the future into the past.
  > A rolling-origin or expanding-window split is the correct form. Grouped data has the same problem: rows from one patient or one user must not straddle the split.
- When the model being tuned is a deep neural network rather than a tree.
  > Model family does not matter.
- When the target classes are heavily imbalanced.
  > Stratified k-fold handles that.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is early stopping, and what is the subtlety?

- Halting training after a fixed number of epochs.
  > A fixed budget is not early stopping.
- [o] Stopping once validation performance stops improving.
  > The best checkpoint is kept. The subtlety is that the epoch count is then itself a hyperparameter tuned on validation, so that score is optimistic. It is a form of regularization and costs nothing extra.
- Stopping once the training loss reaches zero.
  > Zero training loss is usually a sign of overfitting.
- Stopping once the learning rate schedule reaches its minimum.
  > That is a scheduler ending.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do batch, stochastic, and mini-batch gradient descent differ?

- Only in the learning rate each one uses.
  > They differ in how much data each update sees.
- [o] In how many examples each parameter update is computed from.
  > Batch uses the whole dataset (stable, slow), SGD one example (noisy, fast, able to escape shallow minima), mini-batch a subset. Mini-batch dominates because it is what keeps a GPU efficiently utilized.
- SGD is fully deterministic while batch gradient descent is not.
  > The randomness is in SGD's sampling.
- Batch gradient descent cannot be parallelized.
  > It parallelizes well; it is simply expensive per step.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the F1 score, and when is it the wrong summary?

- The arithmetic average of accuracy and recall.
  > It combines precision and recall.
- [o] The harmonic mean of precision and recall.
  > It is the wrong summary when the two errors have very different costs, since it weights them equally by construction. When a false negative costs ten times a false positive, an Fβ score or an explicit cost analysis is more honest.
- The area under the precision-recall curve.
  > That is PR-AUC, a different metric entirely.
- The accuracy measured on the positive class only.
  > That is recall.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What do RMSE, MAE, and R² each tell you?

- They are interchangeable measures of how well a regression fits.
  > They respond differently to outliers and to scale.
- [o] How harshly each one treats a large error, and in what units.
  > RMSE penalizes large errors quadratically in the target's units, MAE weights every error linearly and is more robust, and R² is scale-free. A large gap between RMSE and MAE says a few big errors dominate.
- RMSE suits classification and MAE suits regression.
  > All three are regression metrics.
- R² always falls between 0 and 1.
  > On unseen data a model worse than predicting the mean gives a negative R².`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a parametric versus a non-parametric model?

- Parametric models have hyperparameters and non-parametric ones do not.
  > Both have hyperparameters.
- [o] Whether the parameter count is fixed or grows with the data.
  > A parametric model has a fixed number regardless of data size; a non-parametric one keeps growing, as kNN does by storing every training point. It is why kNN inference scales with the training set and linear regression does not.
- Non-parametric models make no assumptions whatsoever.
  > They make weaker distributional assumptions, not none.
- Parametric models are incapable of overfitting.
  > They can overfit, particularly with many parameters relative to data.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does kNN make a prediction, and what is its main practical cost?

- It fits a small local linear model at each new query point.
  > That is locally weighted regression.
- [o] It votes or averages over the k nearest training points.
  > The cost is that inference scans the training set, and the distance metric degrades in high dimensions. Feature scaling is mandatory, since an unscaled large-magnitude feature dominates the distance.
- It builds a decision tree and traverses it per query.
  > Index structures such as KD-trees accelerate the search; they are not the algorithm.
- It learns a weight per feature by gradient descent.
  > There is no training phase beyond storing the data.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does a support vector machine classify?

- By fitting a probability distribution over each class.
  > That is a generative classifier.
- [o] By maximizing the margin between the classes.
  > The separating hyperplane depends only on the support vectors, and a kernel handles non-linear boundaries implicitly. The kernel trick computes inner products in a high-dimensional space without ever constructing it, which is what makes it tractable.
- By recursively splitting the feature space in two.
  > That is a decision tree.
- By averaging over the nearest labelled examples.
  > That is kNN.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are embeddings, in the general ML sense?

- Losslessly compressed copies of the raw input feature vectors.
  > Compression is incidental; the geometry is the point.
- [o] Dense learned vectors in which distance encodes similarity.
  > They let discrete items such as words, users, and products feed models that need continuous input. It is also why one model's embeddings can power retrieval, clustering, and recommendation without retraining.
- The final output layer of a neural network.
  > That is usually called the output head.
- One-hot encoded representations of categories.
  > One-hot vectors are sparse and carry no similarity structure.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the curse of dimensionality?

- Models simply take longer to train with more features.
  > Compute cost is real but not the phenomenon.
- [o] Data becomes sparse and pairwise distances converge.
  > Volume grows so fast with dimension that every point ends up roughly equidistant, which undermines distance-based methods. It is why kNN and clustering degrade in high dimensions and dimensionality reduction helps them specifically.
- High-dimensional data cannot be visualized directly.
  > A practical inconvenience, not the statistical problem.
- Adding more features always causes overfitting.
  > Overfitting risk rises but is not guaranteed.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does gradient boosting do that a Random Forest does not?

- Sample a random subset of the features at each candidate split.
  > That is a Random Forest's distinguishing move.
- [o] Fit each new tree to the errors the ensemble has left.
  > Boosting reduces bias sequentially; a forest averages independent trees to reduce variance. Boosting typically reaches higher accuracy and is far easier to overfit, so learning rate, depth, and early stopping carry real weight.
- Train all of its trees in parallel.
  > Boosting is inherently sequential.
- Restrict itself to shallow trees.
  > Shallow trees are conventional in boosting but not the defining difference.`}
/>

## Deep learning

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does a single artificial neuron compute?

- A plain weighted sum of its inputs.
  > That is only the first half of it.
- [o] A weighted sum and bias, then a non-linear activation.
  > Without the activation the whole network collapses to one linear map, which is why the non-linearity is essential rather than optional.
- The dot product of two separate input vectors, unweighted.
  > It weights one input vector by learned parameters.
- A probability bounded between 0 and 1.
  > Only with a sigmoid activation; ReLU is unbounded above.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Compare sigmoid, tanh, and ReLU.

- They are broadly interchangeable in practice.
  > Their gradient behavior differs sharply.
- [o] Both sigmoid and tanh saturate, where ReLU does not.
  > Sigmoid squashes to (0,1), tanh is zero-centred but saturates too, and ReLU is cheap and non-saturating for positive inputs at the cost of dead units. Leaky ReLU and GELU keep its benefits while avoiding units stuck at zero.
- ReLU is bounded above and sigmoid is not.
  > It is the other way round.
- Only sigmoid is usable in the hidden layers.
  > ReLU is the default in hidden layers.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why are CNNs well suited to images compared with a fully-connected network?

- They have far more parameters, so they capture much finer detail.
  > They have far fewer, which is part of the advantage.
- [o] Local receptive fields and weight sharing are a correct prior.
  > They encode translation equivariance and cut the parameter count enormously. A fully-connected layer on a 224×224 image would need tens of millions of parameters in the first layer alone.
- They process the pixels one after another.
  > Convolutions apply in parallel across the image.
- They do not need non-linear activation functions.
  > They need them exactly as much as any network.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What do LSTMs and GRUs add over a vanilla RNN?

- Substantially more hidden layers.
  > Depth is orthogonal to the gating mechanism.
- [o] Gates controlling what to keep, forget, and output.
  > They provide a path along which gradients flow without repeated multiplicative shrinkage. GRUs merge some of the LSTM's gates for fewer parameters and often comparable performance.
- Attention across the whole input sequence.
  > Attention is a separate mechanism, added later.
- The ability to run bidirectionally.
  > Any RNN can be made bidirectional.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Describe the transformer architecture at a high level.

- Stacked convolutions with residual connections.
  > Convolutions are not the core operation.
- [o] Stacked self-attention and feed-forward sublayers.
  > Each block wraps multi-head self-attention and a position-wise feed-forward network in residual connections and layer norm, with positional information injected because attention is order-agnostic. Removing recurrence is what lets the whole sequence train in parallel.
- A recurrent encoder feeding a recurrent decoder.
  > That is the pre-transformer seq2seq design.
- An encoder-only network with no feed-forward layers.
  > The feed-forward sublayer holds a large share of the parameters.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does layer normalization differ from batch normalization?

- Layer norm normalizes across the whole batch.
  > That is batch norm.
- [o] Layer norm normalizes across features within one sample.
  > That makes it independent of batch size and comfortable with variable-length sequences, which is why transformers use it; batch statistics are awkward there and with small batches.
- They are exactly equivalent whenever layers are fully connected.
  > They normalize along different axes and give different results.
- Layer norm is applied at inference time only.
  > It is active in both phases, unlike batch norm's running statistics.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What causes exploding gradients, and what is the standard remedy?

- A learning rate that is far too small.
  > That causes slow convergence, not explosion.
- [o] Repeated multiplication by values greater than one.
  > Compounded through depth or time steps, it is remedied by gradient clipping, better initialization, and normalization layers. Clipping by global norm is the routine defence in RNN and transformer training.
- Dropout applied at too high a rate.
  > Dropout does not cause explosion.
- Choosing ReLU as the activation.
  > ReLU mitigates vanishing gradients; it is not a primary cause of explosion.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is transfer learning, and when is full fine-tuning worth it over feature extraction?

- They are two names for one technique.
  > They differ in which parameters are updated.
- [o] Fine-tuning updates the backbone; feature extraction freezes it.
  > Transfer learning is the umbrella: reusing a pretrained model. Full fine-tuning is worth it when you have enough task data and the domain differs from pretraining. On a small dataset it tends to overfit and destroy the pretrained representations.
- Full fine-tuning is always the better option.
  > On small datasets it frequently is not.
- Feature extraction requires retraining the backbone from scratch.
  > It trains only the new head.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is cross-entropy paired with softmax for classification?

- Softmax requires it as a matter of definition.
  > Other losses are computable; they are just worse.
- [o] Their composition gives a clean predicted-minus-target gradient.
  > That avoids the vanishing gradient MSE suffers on a saturated output. It is also why frameworks fuse the two into one numerically-stable operation rather than computing them separately.
- Cross-entropy is much faster to compute.
  > Speed is not the reason.
- Softmax outputs are not valid probabilities without that pairing.
  > Softmax outputs a distribution regardless of the loss.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why subtract the maximum before exponentiating in a softmax?

- To make the outputs sum to exactly one.
  > Division by the sum does that; the shift is separate.
- [o] For numerical stability, since a large logit overflows.
  > Subtracting the max leaves the result mathematically identical while keeping every exponent at most zero. It is why you should use the framework's fused implementation rather than writing softmax by hand.
- To centre the output distribution at zero.
  > Centring is not the goal.
- To make the exponential noticeably cheaper to evaluate.
  > Any speed difference is negligible.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does a "dead ReLU" mean, and what causes it?

- A neuron whose incoming weights have all decayed to zero.
  > Zero weights are unusual and not what the term describes.
- [o] A unit whose pre-activation is negative for every input.
  > It outputs zero and receives no gradient, permanently, usually after too large a learning rate or a bad initialization. Leaky ReLU, ELU, and GELU all keep a small gradient on the negative side to avoid it.
- A neuron switched off by dropout.
  > Dropout is temporary and random.
- A neuron that always outputs its maximum value.
  > ReLU is unbounded above, so there is no maximum to saturate at.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How should the learning rate be scheduled in practice?

- Held fixed for the whole training run.
  > A fixed rate is rarely optimal at both the start and the end.
- [o] A short warmup, then decay on a cosine or step schedule.
  > Early updates stay stable and later ones get fine-grained. Warmup matters most with adaptive optimizers and large batches, where the early moment estimates are unreliable.
- Increased steadily from start to finish.
  > A growing step size destabilizes convergence.
- Set to the largest value that does not immediately produce NaN.
  > That is the divergence boundary, not a good operating point.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What do residual (skip) connections contribute?

- They cut the network's parameter count.
  > Parameters are unchanged.
- [o] A direct path for gradients around each block.
  > Very deep networks stay trainable and each block only has to learn a correction. It is the change that took networks from tens of layers to hundreds, and it appears in every transformer block.
- They remove the need for normalization layers.
  > Residuals and normalization are used together.
- They allow layers to be skipped at inference for speed.
  > The connection is always active.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Compare Xavier (Glorot) and He initialization.

- They are one scheme published under two names.
  > They scale differently.
- [o] He scales up to account for ReLU zeroing half the units.
  > Xavier targets a variance suited to symmetric activations such as tanh. Using Xavier with deep ReLU networks makes activations shrink layer by layer.
- He initialization sets every weight to the same constant.
  > Both draw from a distribution.
- Xavier applies only to convolutional layers.
  > Both apply to any weight matrix.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does batch size interact with the learning rate?

- Larger batches always call for a proportionally smaller learning rate.
  > The relationship usually runs the other way.
- [o] A bigger batch means a lower-variance gradient estimate.
  > That makes a proportionally larger learning rate usable, the linear scaling rule, usually with warmup. Very large batches can still generalize worse, which is why the scaling is a heuristic rather than a law.
- They are entirely independent hyperparameters.
  > They are among the most coupled pair in training.
- Batch size affects memory use and nothing else.
  > Memory is the obvious constraint and not the only effect.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does gradient accumulation achieve?

- It speeds up training on a single GPU device.
  > It is slightly slower per effective batch.
- [o] It simulates a large batch on limited memory.
  > Gradients are summed over several forward and backward passes before the optimizer steps. The subtlety is that batch-norm statistics still use the small physical batch, so results are not identical to a true large batch.
- It stores gradients for later analysis.
  > Nothing is retained after the step.
- It removes the need for an optimizer entirely.
  > The optimizer still applies the accumulated gradient.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is knowledge distillation?

- Removing every weight below some threshold.
  > That is pruning.
- [o] Training a small student to match a larger teacher's outputs.
  > Matching the soft probability distribution rather than hard labels is what carries the extra information: the temperature-softened logits convey the teacher's relative confidence across classes.
- Reducing the numeric precision of the weights.
  > That is quantization.
- Training the same architecture on a random subset of the data.
  > Sampling is unrelated.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is data augmentation effective, and where does it go wrong?

- It adds genuinely new information to the original training dataset.
  > No new information is created; the invariances are what is being taught.
- [o] It teaches invariances through label-preserving variation.
  > That reduces overfitting, and it goes wrong when a transform changes the label, as flipping an image of a digit does. Applying augmentation to the validation set is the other common error, since it makes the measurement inconsistent.
- It reliably improves accuracy either way.
  > A badly chosen transform can hurt.
- It removes any need for other regularization.
  > It is one form of regularization among several.`}
/>

## NLP & LLMs

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is subword tokenization the standard?

- It produces markedly shorter sequences than word-level tokenization.
  > Sequences are usually longer than word-level ones.
- [o] It bounds the vocabulary while still spelling any string.
  > Rare and unseen words decompose into known pieces instead of collapsing to one unknown token. BPE, WordPiece, and SentencePiece are the common algorithms, and they handle morphology and code reasonably too.
- It removes the need for an embedding layer.
  > Tokens are still embedded.
- It guarantees every token is a real morpheme.
  > The splits are learned from frequency, not linguistics.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What distinguishes contextual from static embeddings?

- Contextual embeddings have far more dimensions.
  > Dimensionality is a design choice.
- [o] Whether the vector depends on the surrounding sentence.
  > A static embedding gives one vector per word regardless of usage; a contextual one makes "bank" differ between a river and a loan. word2vec and GloVe are static; representations inside BERT or a GPT-style model are contextual.
- Static embeddings are trained on far more text.
  > Data volume is not the distinction.
- Contextual embeddings need no pretraining at all.
  > They come specifically from pretrained models.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why did transformers replace RNNs for most NLP?

- They get by with far fewer parameters.
  > They typically use many more.
- [o] Removing recurrence lets the whole sequence train in parallel.
  > Attention also gives every position direct access to every other, so a long dependency need not survive many sequential steps. The parallelism is what made training at internet scale practical.
- They need far smaller training datasets to reach the same quality.
  > They are more data-hungry, not less.
- They remove any need for positional information.
  > They need it added explicitly, precisely because attention is order-agnostic.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is parameter-efficient fine-tuning, and what does LoRA do?

- Training the final classification layer only.
  > That is classic feature extraction, one narrow form of the idea.
- [o] Training a small set of added parameters, as LoRA does.
  > LoRA injects trainable low-rank matrices alongside frozen weights, mergeable at inference. It cuts memory and checkpoint size enormously and lets many task-specific adapters share one base model.
- Quantizing the model down to fewer bits.
  > That is quantization, a serving optimization.
- Distilling the weights into a smaller model.
  > Distillation trains a separate student model.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When would you choose RAG over fine-tuning?

- When you need the model to adopt a specific tone and output format.
  > Format and style are what fine-tuning does well.
- [o] When the knowledge is current, changing, or needs a citation.
  > The retrieval corpus updates without touching the weights. The two compose: fine-tune for behavior and format, retrieve for facts.
- When end-to-end latency is the binding constraint.
  > RAG adds a retrieval hop, so it usually costs latency.
- When the available training data is very small.
  > Small data does favour RAG, but currency and attribution are the decisive reasons.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What causes hallucination, and what mitigates it?

- Too few model parameters, so the fix is to scale the model up.
  > Larger models hallucinate more fluently, not less reliably.
- [o] It is trained for plausible continuations, not for truth.
  > It fills gaps confidently. Grounding with retrieval, requiring citations, and constrained decoding all reduce it, and none eliminate it, which is why high-stakes outputs need verification rather than trust.
- A decoding temperature that has been set to zero.
  > Greedy decoding reduces randomness without guaranteeing factuality.
- Errors introduced by the tokenizer.
  > Tokenization is not the source.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do temperature and top-p control generation?

- Temperature sets the maximum length of the output.
  > Length is controlled separately.
- [o] Temperature scales the logits; top-p bounds the candidate pool.
  > Lower temperature is more deterministic, and top-p samples from the smallest set of tokens whose cumulative probability exceeds p, adapting per step. Temperature 0 makes decoding greedy, usually what you want for extraction and classification.
- Top-p restricts the vocabulary to the p most frequent tokens overall.
  > That is top-k; top-p is defined by cumulative probability.
- They are two different names for one parameter.
  > They interact but are distinct.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is attention's cost a bottleneck for long documents?

- Memory use grows linearly with sequence length.
  > Linear growth would not be a bottleneck.
- [o] Time and memory scale quadratically with sequence length.
  > FlashAttention improves the constant and memory pattern; sparse, linear, and sliding-window attention change the asymptotics.
- Positional encodings grow ambiguous at length.
  > Extrapolation is a real issue, but not the cost bottleneck.
- The vocabulary grows with document length.
  > Vocabulary is fixed.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you evaluate an LLM-based feature meaningfully?

- Perplexity measured on a held-out corpus.
  > Perplexity measures language modelling, not task usefulness.
- [o] A task-specific graded eval set, plus a regression suite.
  > Combine automatic metrics, model-based grading, and human review. The fixed regression suite is what stops a prompt change from quietly breaking last month's fix.
- User satisfaction surveys and nothing else.
  > Useful signal, far too slow and coarse to iterate on.
- BLEU score alone, whatever the generation task happens to be.
  > BLEU suits translation and correlates poorly with quality on open-ended generation.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a context window, and why does its size matter?

- The number of documents a RAG system retrieves.
  > Retrieval count is a separate parameter.
- [o] The token budget the model can attend to in one pass.
  > It covers prompt plus generation, bounds what can be supplied in-context, and interacts directly with attention's quadratic cost. Models also use the middle of a long context less effectively, so bigger is not automatically better.
- The size of the model's token vocabulary.
  > Vocabulary is unrelated.
- The number of layers stacked inside the transformer.
  > Depth is a separate dimension.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What objectives are used to pretrain language models?

- Supervised classification over large hand-labelled corpora.
  > Pretraining is self-supervised precisely to avoid needing labels.
- [o] Self-supervised objectives derived from the text itself.
  > Next-token prediction for autoregressive models, masked-token prediction for encoder models. It is what allows training on internet-scale text with no annotation.
- Reinforcement learning from human preferences.
  > RLHF is an alignment stage applied after pretraining.
- Contrastive learning between sentence pairs.
  > Used for embedding models, not general pretraining.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are zero-shot, few-shot, and chain-of-thought prompting?

- Three different names for one and the same underlying prompting technique.
  > They differ in what the prompt supplies.
- [o] Instruction only, instruction plus examples, and reasoning first.
  > Zero-shot gives the instruction alone, few-shot adds worked examples, and chain-of-thought asks for intermediate reasoning. Chain-of-thought helps most on multi-step problems and costs more tokens, a real latency and price consideration.
- Three different sizes of training dataset.
  > None of them involve training.
- Three alternative decoding strategies.
  > Decoding is controlled separately by temperature and top-p.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are the moving parts of a RAG system?

- A single model handed a much longer context window in place of it.
  > A long context is an alternative to RAG, not its implementation.
- [o] A chunked and embedded corpus, an index, a retriever, a generator.
  > Chunk size and overlap, plus a reranker, usually matter more to output quality than the choice of generator.
- A fine-tuned model paired with a cache.
  > Caching is an optimization, not a component of retrieval.
- A vector database and nothing more.
  > The store is one piece of the pipeline.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What are embeddings used for beyond feeding a model?

- Only as the input layer of a model.
  > Their reuse is much of their value.
- [o] Search, clustering, deduplication, and recommendation.
  > All of it works by comparing vectors rather than running the model again, which is why an embedding endpoint is often the most-used part of an LLM stack.
- Compressing the source text for cheaper long-term storage.
  > Embeddings are lossy and not invertible, so they cannot serve as compression.
- Tokenizing the raw input text.
  > Tokenization happens before embedding.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do word2vec and GloVe differ?

- word2vec is contextual while GloVe is a purely static method.
  > Both produce static embeddings.
- [o] Local prediction windows against global co-occurrence counts.

- GloVe needs labelled training data.
  > Both are unsupervised.
- word2vec produces one vector per sentence.
  > It produces one per word.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does chunk size matter in a retrieval pipeline?

- [o] Too small loses context; too large dilutes the embedding.

- It affects retrieval latency and nothing else.
  > Latency changes, but relevance is the bigger effect.
- Smaller chunks are always better for precision.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does a reranker add to a retrieval pipeline?

## ML system design

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When do you choose batch inference over online inference?

  > Model size affects cost, not the fundamental choice.
- [o] When the entities are known ahead of time, so it is a lookup.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What problem does a feature store solve?

- Storing the trained model artifacts.
  > That is a model registry.
- [o] One definition of each feature for both training and serving.
  > An offline store holds point-in-time-correct history, an online store serves low-latency lookups. Without it the same feature gets implemented twice in two languages and quietly diverges, which is training–serving skew.

  > Prediction caching is separate.
- Versioning each training dataset.
  > Data versioning is adjacent but distinct.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is training–serving skew?

- The model performing worse over time.
  > That is drift or degradation.
- [o] The features at serving time differ from those in training.
  > Different code paths, different aggregation windows, or a leaked future value all cause it. It shows up as offline metrics that do not survive contact with production, and it is one of the most common causes of a "good" model failing.
- The gap between training accuracy and held-out test accuracy.
  > That is overfitting.
- Latency differences between environments.
  > Latency is an operational concern.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do data drift, concept drift, and performance degradation differ?

- They are three names for the same thing.
  > They have different causes and different responses.
- [o] Inputs shifted, the input-output relationship shifted, metrics fell.
  > Those are data drift, concept drift, and performance degradation in turn, and either drift can cause the drop. Data drift is detectable without labels; concept drift usually is not, which is why label delay matters so much.
- Concept drift only affects classification.
  > It affects any supervised task.
- Data drift invariably causes model performance to degrade in production.
  > A model can be robust to a shift in a feature it barely uses.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you detect drift when labels arrive weeks late or never?

- [o] Monitor unlabelled proxies against a training baseline.
  > Input feature distributions, the prediction distribution, and model confidence all work. PSI and KL divergence are the usual measures, and a shifting prediction distribution is often the earliest signal available.
- Retrain on a schedule and assume it is fine.
  > Blind retraining can bake in a problem rather than fix it.

  > A useful check, but both can drift together.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a shadow deployment?

  > Staging usually lacks real traffic.

  > That is backtesting.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does a canary deployment differ from shadow testing?

  > It uses real traffic and real users.
- [o] A canary serves real users; shadow serves nobody.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What belongs in a model registry?

  > Weights alone cannot be traced or reproduced.
- [o] Versioned models with their full lineage.

  > Those go to monitoring.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What triggers retraining in a well-run system?

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How does request batching improve GPU throughput?

- [o] It amortizes launch overhead and keeps the GPU saturated.

<MultipleChoice

  > That is pruning.
- [o] Represents weights and activations at lower precision.

- Trains a smaller model to imitate a larger one.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How would you handle cold start in a recommender?

- [o] Fall back to content features and popularity, then collect signal.

- Train one separate model for each user.
  > There is no data to train it on.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Sketch the core of a fraud detection system. What is the defining constraint?

- [o] Extreme imbalance, asymmetric costs, and an adapting adversary.

- [o] Quantifying how far a distribution has moved from baseline.

## MLOps

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

<MultipleChoice

## Statistics, probability & coding for ML

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the difference between a PMF and a PDF?

- A PMF applies to continuous variables.
  > It applies to discrete ones.
- [o] A PMF gives probabilities; a PDF gives a density.
  > A PMF covers discrete outcomes; under a PDF only the area over a region is a probability. It is why a PDF's value can exceed 1 while a PMF's cannot.
- A PDF sums to one across its outcomes.
  > It integrates to one; summation is the discrete case.
- They are the same function published under different names.
  > They are defined for different variable types.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is bootstrapping, and when is it useful?

- Training a model repeatedly on its own earlier predictions.
  > That is self-training.
- [o] Resampling the data with replacement, many times over.
  > It approximates the sampling distribution of a statistic where no closed-form standard error exists. Confidence intervals for a median, an AUC, or any custom metric are the typical uses.
- Initializing weights before training.
  > Unrelated to initialization.
- Removing outliers one round at a time.
  > That is trimming.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is a permutation test?

- A test of whether data is normally distributed.
  > That is a normality test.
- [o] Shuffling the group labels to build the null distribution.
  > The observed statistic is then located within it. The test assumes very little about the distribution, which makes it a good default for an unusual statistic or a small sample.
- A test that permutes the features to measure their importance.
  > Permutation *importance* borrows the idea for a different purpose.
- A non-parametric alternative to bootstrapping.
  > They are related resampling methods answering different questions.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does NumPy vectorization beat a Python \`for\` loop?

- NumPy spreads the work across multiple CPU cores by default.
  > Most element-wise operations are single-threaded.
- [o] The loop runs in compiled C over a contiguous typed array.
  > That avoids per-element Python object overhead and interpreter dispatch. Contiguity also lets the CPU prefetch and vectorize, which is where the remaining factor comes from.
- It simply uses less memory.
  > Arrays are more compact, but speed comes from the execution model.
- It quietly offloads work to the GPU.
  > NumPy is CPU-only; that is what CuPy and framework tensors are for.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is broadcasting in NumPy?

- Copying an array to every worker process.
  > That is a distributed-computing broadcast.
- [o] Expanding size-one dimensions so shapes line up.
  > Arrays of different shapes combine element-wise without the expansion ever being materialized. Subtracting a per-column mean from a matrix is the everyday case: shape \`(n, d)\` against \`(d,)\`.
- Converting an array to a different dtype.
  > That is casting.
- Flattening a multi-dimensional array down to one dimension.
  > That is reshaping.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you find the top-k scores efficiently from n items when k is small?

- Sort the whole array and take the first k.
  > O(n log n) when you do not need the rest ordered.
- [o] \`np.argpartition\` in O(n), then sort only the k selected, or a size-k heap when streaming.
  > The distinction matters when n is millions and k is ten.
- Loop over the array and take the running maximum k separate times.
  > O(nk), fine for tiny k and worse as k grows.
- Deduplicate with a set before sorting.
  > Deduplication is a different operation.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you compute cosine similarity between two vectors in NumPy?

- \`np.dot(a, b)\` on the raw vectors.
  > That is the unnormalized dot product.
- [o] \`np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))\`
  > Pre-normalizing every vector once turns a batch of similarities into a single matrix multiplication, which is how vector search works.
- \`np.corrcoef(a, b)[0, 1]\`, the correlation.
  > Correlation centres both vectors first, which is a different measure.
- \`1 / np.linalg.norm(a - b)\`, an inverse distance.
  > That is a function of Euclidean distance.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why do float32 and float64 matter for ML data?

- float64 always gives better model accuracy.
  > Model accuracy rarely improves, while memory doubles.
- [o] float32 halves memory and is what GPUs are tuned for.
  > Frameworks default to it, and float16 or bfloat16 go further at some numerical risk. Accidentally holding a large dataset as float64 is a common and needless doubling of memory.
- float32 cannot represent negative numbers.
  > It represents the full signed range.
- The difference only affects training runs, never inference.
  > It affects both.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you shuffle features and labels together correctly?

- Call a shuffle on each array separately.
  > That destroys the correspondence between rows and labels.
- [o] Permute the indices once and index both arrays with it.
  > \`sklearn.utils.shuffle\` does exactly this, and setting a seed makes the split reproducible.
- Sort both arrays by an added random column.
  > A workable hack that is easy to get subtly wrong.
- Shuffle only the features, since labels are aligned by position.
  > Alignment by position is precisely what shuffling one side breaks.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is reservoir sampling, and when do you need it?

- Sampling with replacement from a fixed, fully-known dataset.
  > That is bootstrap sampling.
- [o] A uniform sample of k items from a stream, in one pass.
  > The i-th element replaces an existing one with probability k/i. It is the answer when the data does not fit in memory and you cannot count it in advance.
- Sampling in proportion to a per-item weight.
  > That is weighted sampling, a related variant.
- Buffering the whole stream, then sampling.
  > Buffering everything is exactly what it avoids.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does the Central Limit Theorem let an ML engineer do?

- Assume any dataset is normally distributed.
  > The data itself need not be.
- [o] Treat the sampling distribution of a mean as normal.
  > A mean loss or a mean metric across batches qualifies, which is what underpins confidence intervals on evaluation results and gives a measured metric a computable margin of error.
- Justify picking MSE as the loss.
  > MSE follows from a Gaussian noise assumption on the residuals, not from the CLT.
- Safely ignore the outliers.
  > Outliers still affect means, and heavy tails slow convergence to normality.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is selection bias, and how does it reach a training set?

- Choosing the wrong set of features.
  > That is feature selection.
- [o] The training sample does not represent who the model serves.
  > A fraud model trained only on reviewed transactions, or a survey answered only by active users. The model then performs well offline and badly on the population it never saw.
- Sampling the data with replacement.
  > That is bootstrapping.
- Selecting the best-performing model on the test set.
  > That is test-set overfitting, a related but different error.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does correlation not imply causation, and what would establish it?

- Correlation is simply unreliable on small samples.
  > Sample size affects precision, not the logical gap.
- [o] A confounder, reverse causation, or selection can all explain it.
  > Randomized assignment, or a credible quasi-experimental design, is what closes the gap. For a model this often does not matter, until someone proposes intervening on a feature, at which point it matters entirely.
- It does when the correlation exceeds 0.9.
  > Strength does not convert association into causation.
- Only experiments can produce correlations.
  > Observational data produces them routinely.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you compute pairwise Euclidean distances between the rows of a matrix efficiently?

- A nested Python loop over row pairs.
  > It produces the right answer, hundreds of times slower than the vectorized form.
- [o] Expand the square, or call \`scipy.spatial.distance.cdist\`.
  > Both compute every pair in compiled code with one matrix multiplication. The expansion trick can produce small negative values from floating-point error, so clip at zero before taking the square root.
- Sort the rows first, then compare each adjacent neighbour.
  > Sorting does not give all pairwise distances.
- Call \`np.linalg.norm\` on the whole matrix.
  > That returns a single norm, not a distance matrix.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is the complexity of a naive kNN prediction, and how does a heap help?

- O(1), since it just looks up neighbours.
  > Every training point has to be considered.
- [o] O(n log n) sorted, or O(n log k) with a size-k heap.
  > A spatial index or approximate search does better still in low dimensions. At high dimensionality exact indexes lose their advantage, which is why approximate nearest neighbour libraries dominate vector search.
- O(k), independent of the training set size.
  > Cost scales with n.
- O(n²) for every single prediction.
  > That is the cost of all-pairs distances, not one query.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you compute precision and recall from scratch given predictions and labels?

- Precision is correct predictions over total predictions.
  > That is accuracy.
- [o] TP / (TP + FP) and TP / (TP + FN) respectively.
  > One measures how many flagged items were right, the other how many real positives were caught. Guard the denominators: a model that predicts no positives has an undefined precision rather than a zero one.
- Precision works out as TP / (TP + FN).
  > That is recall.
- Recall works out as TN / (TN + FP).
  > That is specificity.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you decode class predictions from a probability matrix?

- Take the row-wise sum of the matrix.
  > Each row already sums to one under softmax.
- [o] \`np.argmax(probs, axis=1)\`, or a threshold per class.
  > Argmax gives the predicted class index; thresholding a chosen class suits an asymmetric decision cost. Argmax implicitly assumes equal error costs, which is a decision worth making explicitly.
- Round each probability to 0 or 1.
  > That can produce several positives or none in a single-label setting.
- \`np.max(probs, axis=1)\` for each row.
  > That returns the confidence, not which class it belongs to.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How do you implement mini-batching for training?

- Slice the array into fixed chunks in order, the same way every epoch.
  > A fixed order lets the model learn the ordering, particularly if the data is sorted by label.
- [o] Shuffle the indices each epoch, then slice the permutation.
  > Decide deliberately whether to drop or keep the final partial batch. Keeping the ragged one is fine unless batch norm is involved, where a batch of one causes trouble.
- Sample rows randomly with replacement each step.
  > That is bootstrap sampling; some rows are seen twice per epoch and others not at all.
- Load the whole dataset and pass it in one batch.
  > That is full-batch gradient descent.`}
