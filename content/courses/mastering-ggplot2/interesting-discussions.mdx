## Where R came from

ggplot2 is an R package, so it inherits a history. **R** was begun in
the early 1990s by **Ross Ihaka** and **Robert Gentleman** at the
University of Auckland, New Zealand. The name is a small double joke: it
plays on their shared initial "**R**," and it nods one letter back from
**S**, the statistical language created by **John Chambers** at Bell
Labs that R was designed to be compatible with. R was released as free
software and grew into the lingua franca of academic statistics, fertile
ground for an ambitious idea like a grammar of graphics to take root.

## A little more on the "2"

The course page told you that **ggplot2** means *grammar of graphics,
version 2*. The detail worth adding: the original `ggplot` (2005) and
ggplot2 (2007) are genuinely *different* implementations, not a version
bump. Wickham found the first design awkward and rewrote it from
scratch with the layered `+` syntax you use today. The original
`ggplot` is long retired; nobody installs it. When people say "ggplot"
in conversation, they almost always mean ggplot2, the "2" has faded
into a quirk of the name.

## The penguins you have been plotting

Several examples in this course use the **Palmer Penguins** data set,
body measurements for three penguin species in Antarctica. It is worth
knowing where it comes from. The measurements were collected by
**Dr. Kristen Gorman** at the **Palmer Station** Long-Term Ecological
Research site in Antarctica. They were packaged into a friendly R data
set by **Allison Horst** as a deliberate, ethically-clean alternative
to *Fisher's iris*, a flower data set long used for teaching, but tied
to its author's promotion of eugenics. Penguins do the same teaching
job (three groups, several numeric measurements that separate them) with
a far better backstory, which is why the visualization community
adopted them so quickly.

## Anscombe's quartet: the case for plotting at all

Of every story in this course, this is the one to remember. In 1973 the
statistician **Francis Anscombe** published four small data sets, now
called **Anscombe's quartet**, engineered to share nearly identical
summary statistics. Each of the four has the *same* mean of x, mean of
y, variance, correlation, and best-fit regression line, to two decimal
places. By the numbers, they are indistinguishable.

Plot them, and they could not be more different.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="r"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.r",
      starterCode: `# 'anscombe' ships with base R. First, the summaries:
# every set has ~the same mean x, mean y, and correlation.
sapply(1:4, function(i) {
  x <- anscombe[[paste0("x", i)]]
  y <- anscombe[[paste0("y", i)]]
  c(mean_x = mean(x), mean_y = round(mean(y), 2),
    cor = round(cor(x, y), 2))
})
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

The three rows are essentially constant across all four sets. Now look
at the pictures:

<CodeBlock
  adapter="r"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.r",
      starterCode: `library(ggplot2)

# Reshape the four x/y pairs into long form, then facet.
long <- do.call(rbind, lapply(1:4, function(i) {
  data.frame(
    set = paste("Set", i),
    x   = anscombe[[paste0("x", i)]],
    y   = anscombe[[paste0("y", i)]]
  )
}))

ggplot(long, aes(x, y)) +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm", se = FALSE, color = "grey70") +
  geom_point(color = "steelblue", size = 2) +
  facet_wrap(~set)
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

Same regression line in every panel, yet one set is a clean linear
trend, one is a curve, one is a perfect line dragged off by a single
outlier, and one is a vertical stack with one far-right point. The
numbers hid all of that. **The plot revealed it instantly.**

<Callout type="info" title="Why this belongs in a ggplot2 course">
Anscombe built his quartet by hand, decades before ggplot2, to make a
single argument: *look at your data.* Summary statistics compress, and
compression discards. A good chart is the cheapest insurance you can buy
against being fooled by an average. Everything you learn here is in
service of that one habit.
</Callout>

## ggplot2 and the tidyverse

ggplot2 is part of the **tidyverse**, a family of R packages, `dplyr`,
`tidyr`, `readr`, and others, that share conventions and a common
philosophy about "tidy" data (one variable per column, one observation
per row). This course keeps such detours minimal; our subject is
ggplot2 itself. But in real projects you will usually see data prepared
with `dplyr`/`tidyr` and then piped straight into `ggplot()`, and the
[Next Steps](/courses/mastering-ggplot2/next-steps) page shows that
pattern.
