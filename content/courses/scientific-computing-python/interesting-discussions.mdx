## The first computer weather forecast

In 1950, a small team led by John von Neumann and the meteorologist
Jule Charney used ENIAC to produce the first computer weather
forecast. They discretized the atmosphere over North America into a
coarse grid, used a simplified set of equations (the *barotropic
vorticity equation*), and ran a 24-hour forecast.

It took about 24 hours to compute, a forecast that finished just in
time to be irrelevant. But it was *qualitatively correct*, and it
launched a research program that, decades later, underlies every
weather app on your phone. The deep lesson was not about speed: it
was that **discretizing a continuous physical field onto a grid and
stepping it forward in time was a viable scientific method**. That
pattern outlasted ENIAC and outlasted FORTRAN, it is still how a
modern supercomputer thinks about the atmosphere.

You met the smallest possible version of this idea, diffusion on a
1-D grid, in the stability discussions. The 1950 forecast was the
same idea at continental scale.

## The hardware ladder

Computational science is uniquely sensitive to hardware. Every time
processors got faster or memory got cheaper, a new class of problem
became tractable.

| Era | Machine | Peak speed | New problems unlocked |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1946 | ENIAC | $5\times10^3$ ops/s | Ballistics, fission |
| 1954 | IBM 704 | $4\times10^4$ ops/s | Weather, FORTRAN |
| 1964 | CDC 6600 | $3\times10^6$ ops/s | Quantum chemistry |
| 1976 | Cray-1 | $1.6\times10^8$ ops/s | 3-D fluid dynamics |
| 1997 | ASCI Red | $1.3\times10^{12}$ ops/s | Full weapon simulation |
| 2008 | Roadrunner | $1.0\times10^{15}$ ops/s | Climate ensembles |
| 2022 | Frontier | $1.1\times10^{18}$ ops/s | Whole-cell biology, quantum-chemical AI |

Each rung is roughly a thousand times faster than the one before,
and each unlocked a research program that ran for a decade or two.

## A Nobel Prize for simulation

The 2013 Nobel Prize in Chemistry went to Martin Karplus, Michael
Levitt, and Arieh Warshel "for the development of multiscale models
for complex chemical systems." The award was not for a new chemical
discovery, it was for the *numerical methods* that let chemists
simulate enzymes on a computer. Computational chemistry and
computational fluid dynamics are now research disciplines in their
own right, the clearest sign that simulation has become a full
partner to theory and experiment.

## William Kahan, the father of floating point

The reason `0.1 + 0.2` behaves identically on your phone, your
laptop, and a supercomputer is a 1985 standard called **IEEE 754**.
Its principal architect, the mathematician **William Kahan**, won the
1989 Turing Award, computing's highest honor, for his work on it,
and is widely called "the father of floating point." Before IEEE 754,
every manufacturer rounded differently, special values like infinity
and NaN were handled inconsistently, and a program that ran correctly
on one machine could silently produce wrong answers on another.

The standard you rely on in every code block of this course is the
quiet reason numerical results are portable at all. The next three
stories are about what happens when its rules are ignored.

## Three disasters caused by rounding error

Floating point is not an abstract concern. Each of these is a
documented failure with real consequences, and each maps onto an idea
from the foundations chapters.

<Callout type="warn" title="The Patriot missile, Dhahran, 1991">
A Patriot missile battery tracked time by counting tenths of a second
in a 24-bit register. But $0.1$ has no exact binary representation,
so each tick carried a tiny rounding error. After about **100 hours**
of continuous uptime, the accumulated drift in the system clock grew
to roughly a third of a second, enough to misplace a fast-moving
target by hundreds of meters. The battery failed to intercept an
incoming Scud missile, and **28 soldiers were killed**. The bug was
*accumulated round-off in a long calculation*, the precision wall,
in its deadliest form.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="The Pentium FDIV bug, 1994">
Intel's Pentium processor shipped with a flaw in the lookup table
used by its floating-point division unit. For certain divisions it
returned wrong results, a famous example being $4195835 / 3145727$,
which came out wrong in about the fifth significant digit. Once the
bug became public, Intel was forced to offer replacements, at a cost
of about **$475 million**. The lesson: even the *hardware* that
implements correctly-rounded arithmetic can be wrong, which is why we
test numerical code against independent references.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warn" title="Ariane 5 Flight 501, 1996">
Forty seconds after launch, the European Space Agency's Ariane 5
rocket veered off course and self-destructed, destroying a payload
worth hundreds of millions of dollars. The cause: a 64-bit
floating-point value (related to the rocket's horizontal velocity)
was converted to a 16-bit signed integer. Ariane 5 flew faster than
its predecessor, the value no longer fit in 16 bits, the conversion
**overflowed**, and the guidance computer shut down. This is the
*range* failure of finite precision, a number that left the
representable interval, rather than a *rounding* failure.
</Callout>

Three failures, three distinct lessons: error accumulates, hardware
can be wrong, and a finite format has edges you can fall off.
Recognizing these failure modes, and writing code that guards
against them, is a large part of what separates a programmer who
uses NumPy from a computational scientist.

## Where to read more

Each of these threads has a vast literature. If you want primary
sources, search for the GAO report on the Patriot failure, the
Lions report on Ariane 5, and Kahan's own writing on IEEE 754, all
freely available, and all far more detailed than these summaries.
The point of this page is not completeness but motivation: the
numerical care this course teaches is not pedantry. People have died
for the lack of it.
