The **context window** is the maximum number of tokens the model can
process in one request: your prompt, the conversation history, any
documents, and the output, all counted together.

<Figure
  slug="llm-context-windows-cutout"
  alt="A long scroll of content fed through a rectangular viewing frame, with the portions at the two ends brightly lit and the middle section noticeably dimmed, and content beyond the frame edges cut off entirely"
/>

As of August 2026, frontier models advertise windows around one
million tokens, with output capped separately at a much lower figure
(commonly around 128,000). Those are two distinct limits and it is
worth keeping them apart.

## The window is a hard boundary

Content outside it does not degrade. It is not there. Whatever the
system around the model does about that (reject the request, drop the
oldest turns, summarize) happens outside the model, and the model has
no way to know something was removed.

This is why a long chat can quietly forget its own beginning. The
interface trimmed the history to fit, and no signal about the
trimming reaches the model or you.

## Filling the window is not free

Two costs, both real.

**Money.** You pay for every input token on every turn. A 500,000-token
document in a ten-turn conversation is five million billed input
tokens unless caching intervenes.

**Attention quality.** This is the one people underestimate.
Performance on retrieving a specific fact is not uniform across
position. The well-documented pattern is often called
**"lost in the middle"**: information at the very start and the very
end of a long context is recalled reliably, while information buried
in the middle is recalled noticeably less well.

The practical consequence: **a bigger window is permission, not a
recommendation.** Pasting an entire 800-page manual because it fits is
usually worse than pasting the three relevant chapters, on both cost
and accuracy.

## Why long context was hard to build

Recall from the attention chapter that every token attends to every
other token, so cost scales with the square of the length. Going from
8,000 to 1,000,000 tokens is a 125-fold increase in length and roughly
15,000-fold in naive attention work.

Against a linear reference, which is what "125 times the text" would cost if
each token were as cheap as the last, the gap is two orders of magnitude:

<Chart slug="attention-cost" />

Getting there required a stack of engineering (attention
approximations, better memory layouts, positional schemes that
extrapolate) rather than one idea. That history is why context length
was the headline capability metric for several years.

<Callout type="info" title="Retrieval versus long context">
With million-token windows available, why bother with retrieval? Cost
and accuracy. Retrieving the five relevant pages and sending 3,000
tokens is cheaper than sending 500,000, and the model attends better to
a short, relevant context than to a long one containing the same facts.
Long context does not make retrieval obsolete; it makes it less
mandatory.
</Callout>
