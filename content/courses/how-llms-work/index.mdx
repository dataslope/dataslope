Not all the way. Nobody can hand you a frontier model to inspect, and

## Why bother

You can use these tools without knowing how they work, in the same way
you can drive without knowing what a differential does. The reason to
learn anyway is that the mechanisms explain the behavior you are
already puzzled by:

* **Why does it hallucinate?** Because it is optimizing for plausible
  continuations, and a plausible continuation of a question is an
  answer, whether or not one is available.
* **Why does it fail at counting letters?** Because it never sees
  letters. It sees tokens, and "strawberry" may be two of them.
* **Why does output cost five times as much as input?** Because
  producing tokens and reading tokens are computationally different
  operations.
* **Why does the same prompt give different answers?** Because the
  final step samples from a distribution rather than picking a maximum.
* **Why do benchmark numbers keep not predicting my results?** Because
  most of them are saturated, contaminated, or measuring something
  other than your task.

Each of those has a mechanical explanation you can hold in your head,
and by the end of this course you will hold all of them.

## Try it right now

Here is a real one. Language models do not see characters, so
character-level questions are hard for them in a way that has nothing
to do with intelligence. Run this to see the same effect from the
other side:

<CodeBlock

text = "strawberry"

print("What you see:  ", list(text))

`,

The model is asked "how many r's are in strawberry" and is looking at
two opaque symbols. It answers from what it has read *about* the word,
not from inspecting it, because inspecting it is not an operation
available to it.

## What you will build

Everything in this course runs in your browser. No API key, no
account, no cost.

* A **byte-pair encoding tokenizer** from scratch, trained on text you
  provide.
* An **embedding similarity** search, to see what "meaning as
  geometry" actually means.
* A **sampler** with temperature and top-p, so you can watch
  randomness enter the system.
* A **cost calculator** that prices a real conversation, including
  what caching and batching do to the bill.
* A small **evaluation harness**, because the last chapter's argument
  is that you should stop reading leaderboards and measure your own
  task.

## What you need

Comfort reading basic Python: loops, dictionaries, functions. No
machine learning background, no linear algebra beyond "a vector is a
list of numbers", and no calculus at all.

<Callout type="info" title="What this course deliberately skips">
There is no backpropagation, no training loop, and no gradient
descent. This is a course about what a *trained* model does when you
send it a prompt, which is the part that affects your daily work. If
you want the training side afterwards,
[Machine Learning with scikit-learn](/courses/machine-learning-scikit-learn)
is the natural next step.
</Callout>

## A note on numbers

Prices, model names, context sizes, and benchmark scores in this
course are marked with **August 2026** where they appear. They will
age. The *structure* around them (output costing several times input,
cached input costing about a tenth, benchmarks saturating and being
replaced) has held for years and is what the exercises actually teach.
