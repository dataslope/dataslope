Every model launch comes with a table of benchmark scores. This
chapter explains what those tests are, so the next chapter can explain
why the table tells you less than it appears to.

## The main families

### Knowledge and reasoning

**MMLU** (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) is 57 subjects of
multiple-choice questions, from elementary mathematics to professional
law. It was the standard general-knowledge benchmark for years.

<Figure
  slug="llm-what-benchmarks-measure-inline-cutout"
  alt="A standard test rig with one machine scoring well on it while a second bench of real tasks stands untouched"
  caption="A benchmark measures the benchmark; your task is a separate question."
/>

By 2026 it is **saturated**. Every frontier system scores above 92%,
and documented label errors in the dataset cap the achievable score
near 95%. A benchmark where everyone scores within a few points of
each other and of the ceiling cannot distinguish anything.

**GPQA Diamond** was the successor for hard reasoning: graduate-level
biology, chemistry, and physics questions written so that non-expert
PhD holders with web access score only around 34%. For a while it
separated models across the 60% to 90% range. By October 2026 it has
gone the way of MMLU: the leading systems score around 95%, and about
two dozen of the models one public leaderboard tracks score above
90%.[^vals-gpqa]

### Coding

**SWE-bench Verified** was the important one, and what happened to it
is the most instructive story in this chapter. It presents real GitHub
issues from real repositories and requires a patch that makes the
repository's own tests pass. Because success is checked by running
code rather than by matching text, it was long considered hard to
contaminate.

It was not hard enough. In February 2026 OpenAI stopped reporting it,
after finding that frontier models from several labs could reproduce
some of its reference fixes from memory, and that most of the hard
tasks its own models kept failing had tests that rejected correct
fixes.[^openai-verified] It recommended **SWE-bench Pro** instead, then
withdrew that recommendation in July 2026 when an audit found roughly
30% of Pro's tasks broken.[^openai-pro]

The task shape is still the right one: read a codebase, make a
multi-file change, verify it. That is why repository-patch benchmarks
remain the best predictor of agentic coding ability. What changed is
how much trust any single public set of them deserves. Running the
tests guards against matching text, but not against a model that has
already seen the patch, and not against a test that is itself wrong.

### Abstract reasoning

**ARC-AGI-2** presents grid puzzles where you infer a transformation
rule from a few examples and apply it to a new case. Tasks are
adversarially filtered so that memorized pattern-matching does not
solve them, and the evaluation set is kept private. It launched in
2025 with real headroom above every model, and the best verified
scores passed 90% during 2026.[^arc-opus5]

Its successor, **ARC-AGI-3**, launched in March 2026 and changes the
format rather than only the difficulty. Instead of a static grid, the
system is dropped into a small interactive game and has to work out
the goal and the rules by playing it. Every frontier model scored under
1% at launch.[^arc3] By September 2026 the best result on ARC Prize's
standard harness was 62.7%, which still makes it one of the few
benchmarks measuring something with room to grow.[^arc-astra]

### Contamination-resistant suites

Because the older benchmarks leak into training data, several
evaluations are designed to resist it:

* **LiveBench** refreshes with new problems monthly.
* **LiveCodeBench** filters problems by submission date, so only
  problems published after a model's cutoff count.
* **Humanity's Last Exam (HLE)** keeps a private holdout set.
* **FrontierMath** uses expert-authored problems that are never
  published.

The shared design principle: **the answers must not be findable in the
training corpus.**

Resisting contamination does not stop a benchmark from being outgrown.
HLE was released in January 2025 with every frontier model scoring
under 10%; by September 2026 the best published scores were around
60%.[^hle]

## How to read a score

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `# Whether a gap is meaningful depends on the ceiling and the spread.
benchmarks = {
    "MMLU":         {"model_a": 0.923, "model_b": 0.917, "ceiling": 0.95},
    "GPQA Diamond": {"model_a": 0.960, "model_b": 0.954, "ceiling": 1.00},
    "ARC-AGI-3":    {"model_a": 0.627, "model_b": 0.302, "ceiling": 1.00},
}

for name, r in benchmarks.items():
    gap = r["model_a"] - r["model_b"]
    headroom = r["ceiling"] - r["model_a"]
    share = gap / headroom if headroom > 0.001 else float("inf")
    print(f"{name:<14} gap {gap:+.3f}  headroom {headroom:.3f}  "
          f"gap is {share:.0%} of remaining headroom")

print()
print("The same 0.6 point gap means very different things depending")
print("on how much room is left above it.")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

A one-point gap near a ceiling is noise. The same gap in the middle of
a wide range is a real difference. Always look at where on the scale
the comparison sits.

<Callout type="info" title="What the numbers do not include">
Benchmarks measure capability under ideal conditions: a clean prompt, a
well-formed problem, no adversarial input, no ambiguity about what
counts as success. Your production traffic has none of those
properties, which is why benchmark rank and production performance
correlate loosely at best.
</Callout>

  > Every frontier system scores above 92% against a ceiling near 95%, leaving no room to separate them. GPQA Diamond reached the same state in 2026.
- Models have memorized the entire test set.
  > Contamination is a separate concern from saturation.
- The benchmark has been withdrawn by its authors.
  > It remains published and widely reported.

  > A 0.6 point difference supports no such claim.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What does ARC-AGI-3 change compared with ARC-AGI-2?

- It switches to multiple-choice questions for faster scoring.
  > It moved away from static answers, not toward multiple choice.
- [o] Systems must learn interactive games by playing them.
  > Instead of answering a static grid puzzle, the system explores an unfamiliar game and works out its goal and rules, which every frontier model failed at launch.
- It adds questions about recent events to block memorization.
  > It has no knowledge component at all.
- It has human judges score each answer by hand.
  > Progress through a game is checked automatically.

When a benchmark saturates, the useful successor changes what is
measured, not only how hard it is.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why do non-expert PhD holders score only about 34% on GPQA Diamond?

- The scoring penalizes incorrect answers heavily.
  > Scoring is straightforward accuracy.
- [o] The questions require expertise in the specific subfield, even with web access.
  > They were constructed so that general scientific training plus search is insufficient, which is what makes the benchmark discriminating.
- The time limit is too short to answer carefully.
  > The difficulty is not a timing artifact.
- The questions are deliberately ambiguous.
  > They have definite correct answers.

The 34% figure is the design target that establishes the difficulty
level.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Which benchmark best predicts agentic coding performance?

- [o] A SWE-bench-style task, since its shape matches the job.
  > Reading a codebase, making a multi-file change, and passing the project's tests is what the work involves, though any one public set of such tasks can be contaminated.
- ARC-AGI-3, since adapting to a new environment is what agents do.
  > Learning a puzzle game shares little structure with codebase work.
- GPQA Diamond, since it measures hard scientific reasoning.
  > It measures scientific reasoning rather than software work.
- MMLU, since it covers computer science topics.
  > Multiple-choice knowledge questions do not resemble the task.

Predictive validity comes from the task resembling the job.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What conditions do benchmarks assume that production traffic lacks?

- [o] Clean prompts, well-formed problems, and unambiguous success criteria.
  > Real traffic is ambiguous, messy, and often lacks a definite right answer, which is why benchmark rank transfers poorly.
- Access to the internet during evaluation.
  > Some benchmarks permit search and others do not.
- Unlimited token budgets.
  > Budgets vary by evaluation protocol.
- A single attempt per problem with no retries.
  > Retry policies differ across benchmarks.

Ideal conditions are what makes a benchmark measurable and what makes
it unrepresentative.`}
