Benchmarks are useful. Benchmark *tables in launch announcements* are
a different thing, and reading them well requires knowing four failure
modes.

<Figure
  slug="llm-benchmark-pitfalls-cutout"
  alt="A podium and scoreboard where a hidden pipe feeds answers directly to one contestant from behind, and a fresh sealed envelope of unseen questions sits unopened to one side"
/>

## 1. Saturation

Covered already, and worth restating as a rule: **a benchmark stops
being informative once everyone scores near its ceiling.** MMLU is the
canonical case. The frontier moved past what the test can measure, and
the reported numbers now differ by less than the measurement error.

The tell is easy to spot. If every model in the table is within three
points of every other, the benchmark is not distinguishing them.

## 2. Contamination

Benchmark questions and answers get published. Published text gets
crawled. Crawled text becomes training data. A model that saw the test
answers during training scores well without having the capability the
test was designed to measure.

<Figure
  slug="llm-benchmark-pitfalls-inline-cutout"
  alt="A machine scoring perfectly on a test rig whose answer card is visibly already inside the machine"
/>

This is rarely deliberate and is very hard to rule out, because
nobody can fully audit a multi-trillion-token corpus for overlap with
every benchmark.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `# A contaminated benchmark and a clean one can look identical
# from the outside. What separates them is not visible in the score.
def looks_capable(memorized_answers, genuine_capability):
    return "high score" if (memorized_answers or genuine_capability) else "low score"

cases = [
    ("memorized the test set", True, False),
    ("genuinely capable",      False, True),
    ("both",                   True, True),
    ("neither",                False, False),
]

print(f"{'situation':<26}{'reported result':>16}")
for label, mem, cap in cases:
    print(f"{label:<26}{looks_capable(mem, cap):>16}")

print()
print("Rows 1 and 2 are indistinguishable from the score alone.")
print("Only a held-out or post-cutoff test can separate them.")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

The defenses are the contamination-resistant suites from the previous
chapter: private holdouts, date filtering, monthly refreshes,
unpublished problems.

## 3. Selective reporting

A launch announcement chooses which benchmarks to show. Nobody is
obliged to publish the ones where they placed third.

The diagnostic questions:

* Are the *standard* benchmarks for this capability present, or an
  unfamiliar selection?
* Are the comparison models current, or an older version of a
  competitor?
* Is the evaluation configuration disclosed? The same model at
  different reasoning-effort settings can differ by many points, and
  a comparison that runs one model at maximum effort against another
  at default is not a comparison of models.

That last one matters more since reasoning effort became tunable. Two
numbers produced under different budgets are not measuring the same
thing.

## 4. Construct mismatch

The deepest problem, and the least discussed. A benchmark measures
performance on the benchmark. Whether that transfers to your task
depends entirely on whether your task resembles it.

<Figure
  slug="llm-benchmark-pitfalls-1-saturation-inline-cutout"
  alt="A test rig whose gauge is pinned hard at its top stop, three different machines all reading the same"
/>

SWE-bench predicts agentic coding reasonably well because the task
shapes match. It predicts almost nothing about how well the model will
extract fields from your invoices, because nothing about that
resembles fixing a GitHub issue.

<Callout type="warn" title="The practical consequence">
A model that ranks third on every public leaderboard can be the best
model for your specific task, and you will never learn that from the
leaderboards. The only way to find out is to test on your own data,
which is the next chapter.
</Callout>

## Reading a launch table well

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `checks = [
    ("Is every model within ~3 points?", "saturated, ignore this row"),
    ("Is the benchmark contamination-resistant?", "if not, discount the score"),
    ("Are the standard benchmarks present?", "if not, ask what is missing"),
    ("Are competitor versions current?", "stale comparisons inflate the gap"),
    ("Is the effort or config disclosed?", "undisclosed means not comparable"),
    ("Does the task resemble yours?", "if not, the score does not transfer"),
]

print("Reading a benchmark table:")
print()
for i, (question, implication) in enumerate(checks, 1):
    print(f"{i}. {question}")
    print(f"   -> {implication}")
    print()

print("If four or more raise concerns, the table is marketing, not evidence.")
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

  > A model that saw the answers scores well without having the capability the test measures.
- Multiple models being tested on the same hardware.
  > Shared infrastructure does not affect scores.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`How can you tell a contaminated result from a genuinely capable one?

- Contaminated models score suspiciously close to 100%.
  > Contaminated scores can sit anywhere.
- [o] You cannot, from the score alone; it requires a held-out or post-cutoff test.

  > This is suggestive evidence and not reliable on its own.

<MultipleChoice

- The models are all equally capable in general.
  > It says nothing about capability beyond this test.
- [o] The benchmark is saturated and not distinguishing them.
  > When the spread is smaller than the measurement error, the row carries no information.

  > Correct administration produces this pattern once a test is saturated.

Tight clustering near a ceiling is the signature of a benchmark that

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What is construct mismatch?

- [o] A benchmark measuring something that does not resemble your task.
  > A high SWE-bench score predicts agentic coding and says almost nothing about extracting fields from invoices.
- Using a benchmark whose questions contain factual errors.
  > That is a label-quality problem.
- Comparing models trained on different data distributions.
  > Training differences are expected and are not the issue.
- Running a benchmark on hardware it was not designed for.
  > Hardware does not affect what a benchmark measures.

The score is real; its relevance to your work is the open question.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice

  > Aggregate rank averages across tasks unlike yours, so only testing on your own data can settle it.
- It is likely contaminated on the benchmarks it leads.
  > Rank position is not evidence about contamination.
- It is unsuitable for production use.
  > Rank does not determine suitability.
- It will rank third for your task as well.
  > Leaderboard rank transfers poorly to specific tasks.

Public rank is an average over tasks that are not yours.`}

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is contamination difficult to rule out?

- Benchmarks change their questions too frequently.
  > Most benchmarks are stable, which is part of the problem.
- [o] Auditing a multi-trillion-token corpus against every benchmark is impractical.
  > The scale makes exhaustive overlap detection infeasible even with full access.
- Contamination only appears after a model is deployed.
  > It is introduced during training.
- Providers refuse to disclose their training data.
  > Disclosure would help and is not the only obstacle.

Scale, not secrecy, is the primary barrier.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Which question most directly tests whether a benchmark score matters to you?

- How many models have been evaluated on it?
  > Popularity does not establish relevance to your task.
- Was the benchmark published in a peer-reviewed venue?
  > Publication venue speaks to rigor rather than relevance.
- [o] Does the benchmark's task resemble what you actually need done?
  > Transfer depends on task-shape similarity, which is the construct-validity question.
- Was the benchmark released after the model's training cutoff?
  > Important for contamination, and it does not address relevance.

Relevance is about similarity of task, and it is the question people
