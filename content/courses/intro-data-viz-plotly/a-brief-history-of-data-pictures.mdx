## The first picture of disagreement

A map is the oldest visual abstraction. A line on a map is not a
river, it is a *symbol* for a river. That gap, between *the thing*
and *the picture of the thing*, is the whole foundation of data
visualization, and humans have been exploiting it for a very long
time. The oldest known *star* map is carved into a mammoth tusk
roughly 32,000 years old.

But the first true *statistical* chart came much later. In **1644**,
the Flemish astronomer **Michael van Langren** was wrestling with a
fierce open problem: the longitude difference between Toledo and
Rome. Twelve astronomers had published estimates ranging from 17° to
30°. Instead of averaging them into one false-confident number, van
Langren drew each estimate as a dot on a single horizontal line. Here
are his twelve values, with one thing he could not have added:

<Chart slug="langren-longitude-1644" />

Just by *seeing* the dots spread out, you feel the uncertainty in your
gut. Van Langren had discovered something profound: **a picture of
disagreement is often more honest than a single number.**

The modern reader gets a second lesson for free. We know the answer
now, and **every one of the twelve is too big**, the closest by more
than a degree. Their disagreement was visible to van Langren; their
shared bias was not visible to anyone, because a systematic error
that everybody makes looks exactly like data.

## Playfair invents the charts you already know

If van Langren is the great-grandfather of statistical graphics, the
*father* is **William Playfair**, a Scottish engineer and political
economist. In a single 1786 book, the *Commercial and Political
Atlas*, Playfair invented or popularized the **line chart** (for
change over time) and the **bar chart** (for comparing categories).
A few years later, in 1801, he added the **pie chart**.

<Figure
  slug="viz-history-playfair-cutout"
  alt="An engraver's bench with a copper plate and burin, a press, and a printed sheet carrying one row of bars and one pie"
  caption="William Playfair, 1786 and 1801: the bar chart, then the pie, engraved for readers who only trusted tables."
/>

Playfair's leap was philosophical. To a generation raised on tables
of numbers, the claim that *economic data could be seen, not just
read* was strange and a little suspicious. A bar chart was a
*picture* of trade, was that serious? Today the answer is obvious;
in 1786 it was not. Every chart you make in this course descends
from his argument that pictures of data are a legitimate way to know
things.

## Florence Nightingale: a chart that changed a war

The most consequential early chart was not made by a mathematician.
In 1854, an English nurse named **Florence Nightingale** arrived at
a British military hospital in Scutari during the Crimean War. What
she found was horror, wounded soldiers were dying in vast numbers,
but not, as the public believed, of their wounds. They were dying of
**typhus, cholera, and dysentery contracted inside the hospital
itself.**

<Figure
  slug="viz-history-nightingale-cutout"
  alt="A hospital ward of low beds, and a large round diagram of wedges of different lengths standing on an easel"
  caption="Scutari, 1854: Florence Nightingale's soldiers were dying of the hospital, and a diagram is what proved it."
/>

Nightingale knew the numbers better than anyone in London. She also
knew that *numbers in a report do not change policy*. So she did
something almost unprecedented for a woman, or for anyone, in the
mid-19th century: **she drew a chart.**

The chart she published in 1858 is now famous as the **Coxcomb** or
**rose diagram**. Each month of the war is a wedge fanning out from a
center; the *area* of each wedge encodes the number of deaths, and
the *color* encodes the cause, blue for preventable disease, red
for wounds. The blue wedges were *enormous*. The red wedges were
tiny. Anyone who looked, a politician, a queen, a newspaper editor,
could see in a single glance that **soldiers were not dying of war;
they were dying of dirt.**

The chart did its job. The British government created a Sanitary
Commission, the hospitals were cleaned, and mortality plummeted. In
1859, Nightingale became the **first woman elected a Fellow of the
Royal Statistical Society.** Her diagram is the first widely-cited
example of data visualization as an instrument of political change,
and every public-health chart since traces a line back to it.

<Callout type="info" title="The honesty-vs-impact tension, in one chart">
Modern specialists point out that a plain **bar chart** would have
been *more accurate* than the rose diagram, because the eye judges
length better than area. They are right. But Nightingale's chart was
also **persuasive design**, the dramatic rose shape was memorable,
and it *traveled*. Sometimes the best chart for *exploration* is not
the best chart for *advocacy*. We return to this tension throughout
the course.
</Callout>

Two contemporaries deserve a sentence each, because they round out
the founding generation. In **1854**, physician **John Snow** mapped
cholera deaths around the Broad Street pump in London, a founding
act of data-driven, spatial epidemiology. And in **1869**, the
engineer **Charles Minard** drew a flow map of Napoleon's
catastrophic 1812 Russian campaign that Edward Tufte would later
call "probably the best statistical graphic ever drawn."

## From parliamentary foldouts to your browser

For most of this history, charts were **static**. Playfair's lines
were ink on paper; Nightingale's rose was a foldout in a
parliamentary report; Excel charts, even in the 1990s, were small
frozen pictures. A static chart says one thing: *"Here is what I, the
author, decided was important."* You could look at it, you could not
*ask it anything*.

<Figure
  slug="viz-a-brief-history-of-data-pictures-from-parliamentary-foldouts-inline-cutout"
  alt="A large folded paper chart opened out on a table beside a small glowing panel showing the same shape"
/>

That changed when the chart moved onto the **web**. Two browser
technologies, **SVG** (shapes that respond to mouse and touch) and
**JavaScript** (a language that reacts to input in real time), made
every chart on every page *interactive-capable*. In 2011, **Mike
Bostock** released **D3.js**, the JavaScript library that powered a
decade of New York Times graphics. D3 was extraordinarily powerful
and extraordinarily *hard*, a single chart could take hundreds of
lines.

So the next decade was spent building friendlier wrappers around
that engine. The one this course uses, **Plotly Express**, which we
meet properly on the next page, turns an interactive web chart into
a single line of Python.

## Interactivity is not a gimmick

It is tempting to think of interactivity as a *nice extra*. It is
not. A truly interactive chart changes the **cognitive workload** of
analysis. Ben Shneiderman captured the idea in a famous 1996 mantra:

> **Overview first, zoom and filter, then details on demand.**

You see the *whole shape* of the data, then drill into the part that
interests you, then read exact values, and each step is a tiny
question you ask *without writing code*. That loop, done in seconds,
is the rhythm of modern data analysis. This entire course runs in
your browser precisely so you can *live inside that loop* on every
page.

Here is a first taste. This is the kind of analysis Nightingale was
doing, comparing causes over time, rendered with the tool you are
about to learn. Don't worry about the syntax yet; just **hover** over
the bars and **click a legend entry** to hide a series.

<CodeBlock

import plotly.express as px

# A small table inspired by Nightingale's: monthly deaths by cause.
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "month": ["Jan", "Feb", "Mar", "Apr", "May", "Jun"] * 2,
    "cause": ["Disease"] * 6 + ["Wounds"] * 6,
    "deaths": [320, 410, 540, 480, 350, 290, 40, 55, 60, 65, 50, 45],
})`,
      starterCode: `fig = px.bar(
    df,
    x="month",
    y="deaths",
    color="cause",
    barmode="group",
    template="simple_white",
    title="Monthly deaths by cause, a modern Nightingale chart",

)
fig.show()
`,
    },

You did not need to know any Python to *read* that chart, the blue
"Disease" bars dwarf the red "Wounds" bars, exactly Nightingale's
point. **A well-made chart explains itself.** That is the standard
we are aiming for, and the next page introduces the tool that gets
us there in one line.
