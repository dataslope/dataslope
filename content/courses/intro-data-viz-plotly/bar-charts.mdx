The **bar chart** is the most-used chart type in the world for a
reason: it answers the most common question in business and
science, *how do these categories compare?*, using the most
accurate encoding the human eye has access to.

<Figure
  slug="viz-bar-charts-cutout"
  alt="Bar charts, an isometric illustration"
/>

That accuracy holds however many bars you draw, which is exactly the trap: the
encoding stays perfect long after the chart has stopped being readable.

<Chart slug="too-many-bars" />

## When to reach for a bar chart

A bar chart is the right choice when:

- You are comparing values across a **set of discrete categories**.
- The values are **quantitative** (counts, dollars, percentages).
- You want the reader to **rank** or **compare** the categories.

Examples:

- Sales per region.
- Tickets sold per movie.
- Population per country.
- Average score per student group.

If your x-axis is *time* (with many points in regular intervals),
prefer a **line chart**. If you are showing a *distribution* of a
single variable, prefer a **histogram**. Bar charts are for
**comparing discrete categories**.

<Figure
  slug="viz-bar-charts-inline-cutout"
  alt="A row of standing blocks of different heights on a common base rail, with one flat rule laid across their tops to compare them"
  caption="Bars share a baseline, so the eye compares their lengths precisely."
/>

There is one common case where the answer is "not a bar chart": many
categories whose values all sit in a narrow band.

<Chart slug="dot-plot-beats-bar" />

Most of every bar there is the stretch all twenty categories have in common,
which carries no information. Replacing each bar with a dot at its end keeps
the comparison and drops four fifths of the ink, and because a dot encodes
position rather than length, the axis is then free to be cropped to where the
data actually is.

What the cropped version gives up is absolute size. A dot at 91% on an axis
running from 65 to 95 no longer shows you that 91% is most of the way to
everything, so this is the wrong swap when "how big" is the question rather
than "which, and by how much".

There is also a case where the standard advice, "sort by value", is the wrong
move:

<Chart slug="sorted-vs-natural-order" />

Sorting by value is the right default, because an arbitrary order wastes
position, which is the strongest thing a chart has. It stops being right the
moment the categories already carry an order of their own: shirt sizes, age
brackets, school years, months, and any scale running from "strongly disagree"
to "strongly agree".

The test is one question. If two neighbouring categories swapped places, would
anything be wrong? If yes, the order is data and sorting deletes it.

## The simplest bar chart

Before the code, one thing bars do for free that is easy to spend money on
instead. When bars fall into groups, the spacing can say so on its own:

<Chart slug="proximity-groups-bars" />

No borders, no background bands, no second color, no group name over each
cluster. Two spacings. That leaves color free to carry a second variable
rather than being spent on grouping you could have had for nothing.

Proximity is the cheapest encoding on the shelf, because it adds no ink at
all, and the same principle governs the rest of a chart's furniture: a label
nearer its axis than its title belongs to the axis. Distance is already saying
something. The only question is whether it is saying what you meant.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import pandas as pd
import plotly.express as px

df = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "team": ["Marketing", "Engineering", "Sales", "Support"],
        "people": [12, 47, 28, 19],
    }
)

fig = px.bar(
    df,
    x="team",
    y="people",
    template="simple_white",
    title="Headcount by team",
    labels={"team": "Team", "people": "Headcount"},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

That's the canonical Plotly Express call. Note:

- `x="team"` puts categories on the x-axis.
- `y="people"` makes the bar heights encode the count.
- The bars share a common baseline (zero), which is what makes
  comparison easy.

<Figure
  slug="viz-bar-charts-simplest-bar-chart-inline-cutout"
  alt="Four standing blocks of different heights on one base rail and nothing else in the frame"
  caption="One category per bar, one number per height, and nothing else needed."
/>

## Sort by value, almost always

<Chart slug="sorted-bars" />

A bar chart in *alphabetical* order is almost always worse than one
sorted by value. Sorting lets the eye read the *ranking* in zero
time.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import pandas as pd
import plotly.express as px

df = pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "team": ["Marketing", "Engineering", "Sales", "Support"],
        "people": [12, 47, 28, 19],
    }
).sort_values("people", ascending=False)

fig = px.bar(
    df,
    x="team",
    y="people",
    template="simple_white",
    title="Headcount by team (sorted)",
    labels={"team": "Team", "people": "Headcount"},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

The only time you should *not* sort by value is when the categories
have a **natural order**:

- Months: Jan, Feb, ..., Dec
- Ordinal categories: small, medium, large
- Years on a time axis

## Horizontal bars for long labels

The same nine categories both ways. Only the axis changed:

<Chart slug="horizontal-bars-long-labels" />

When category labels are long, **horizontal bars** are far more
readable than vertical bars with rotated labels.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import plotly.express as px

df = px.data.gapminder().query("year == 2007 and continent == 'Europe'")
df = df.sort_values("lifeExp")

fig = px.bar(
    df,
    x="lifeExp",
    y="country",
    orientation="h",
    template="simple_white",
    title="European life expectancy in 2007",
    labels={"lifeExp": "Life expectancy (years)", "country": "Country"},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

The pattern: `orientation="h"` plus swap the x and y arguments.
Notice the bars are sorted *ascending*, for horizontal bars, that
puts the largest at the top, which matches how we read top-to-
bottom.

## Grouped bars: comparing across two categories

When each "category" also has a *sub-category*, use **grouped bars**
with `color="..."` and `barmode="group"`.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import plotly.express as px

df = px.data.tips()

avg = df.groupby(["day", "sex"], as_index=False)["tip"].mean()

fig = px.bar(
    avg,
    x="day",
    y="tip",
    color="sex",
    barmode="group",
    template="simple_white",
    title="Average tip by day and sex",
    labels={"tip": "Mean tip (USD)", "day": "Day", "sex": "Sex"},
    category_orders={"day": ["Thur", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"]},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

`category_orders={...}` is a small but invaluable trick, it forces
the days into chronological order instead of alphabetical.

Grouped and stacked are the same twelve numbers, and the choice decides
which comparison is easy and which is nearly impossible:

<Chart slug="grouped-vs-stacked-bars" />

## Stacked bars: parts of a whole, across categories

Use `barmode="stack"` when each category is a *total* you want to
break into parts.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import plotly.express as px

df = px.data.tips()

avg = df.groupby(["day", "sex"], as_index=False)["tip"].sum()

fig = px.bar(
    avg,
    x="day",
    y="tip",
    color="sex",
    barmode="stack",
    template="simple_white",
    title="Total tips by day, stacked by sex",
    labels={"tip": "Total tips (USD)", "day": "Day"},
    category_orders={"day": ["Thur", "Fri", "Sat", "Sun"]},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warn" title="Be careful with stacked bars">
Stacked bars are great for showing **totals** but **bad** for
comparing the segments *across* categories. The bottom segments
share a baseline (easy to compare), but the upper segments float
at different heights (much harder to compare). If sub-category
comparison is the main point, use grouped bars instead.
</Callout>

Two things a stack quietly asserts are worth checking before you draw one. The
first is that the parts are mutually exclusive and exhaustive:

<Chart slug="stack-that-double-counts" />

Multi-select survey answers satisfy neither, and the axis running to 187% is
the tell. The fix is not to normalise, which would make the arithmetic *look*
right while making every number wrong; it is to draw plain bars from a common
zero.

The axis is the tell, but the eye has done the wrong thing before it gets
there, because a stack is read as a *composition*: each segment looks like a
share of the bar, so SQL at 41% of respondents renders as about 22% of the
total length. The segments are not comparable to each other either. Their
lengths are right, but drawn end to end each one starts wherever the last
finished, so equal adoption reads as unequal because only the first segment
has a baseline.

The second is what normalising to 100% throws away:

<Chart slug="percent-of-what" />

The Government bar has the most dramatic mix on the chart, and that is not a
finding. At n = 30 the sampling noise on a share is around 9 percentage
points, so a bar that looks like a different population is roughly what you
would expect even if every group were identical. Width is one of the two
standard fixes, and it has a second virtue: the area of each color is now the
actual count, so the eye adds up instead of averaging five percentages as
though they weighed the same. The other fix is to print n under each bar and
let the reader discount the small ones.

Per cent, per capita, per thousand and index-to-100 all discard the
denominator, and a chart that has discarded its denominator cannot tell you
which of its numbers to believe.

## Common bar chart mistakes

The first one is the axis. A bar's length *is* its value, so the moment the
axis stops at something other than zero, the two have come apart:

<Chart slug="bar-truncation" />

And here is that crop, drawn:

<Chart slug="bar-truncation-cropped" />

The rule has an edge worth knowing, because "always start at zero" is often
repeated as though it applied to every chart. It does not, and nothing about
the axis differs between these two panels. The mark does:

<Chart slug="zero-baseline-bars-not-lines" />

A bar says its value with a *length* measured up from the baseline, and that
is all a bar is, so moving the baseline to 92 means every length is the value
minus 92 and a reader comparing two bars is comparing two differences while
believing they are comparing two values. A line says its value with a
*position*, and invites you to compare it with the next position along, which
is a slope. A truncated axis exaggerates that slope, which is worth watching,
but it hands nobody a wrong ratio, because nothing in a line chart is measured
from the bottom of the frame.

Zero is mandatory when the mark's *length* is the value, and optional when it
is not. Bars, columns and areas: zero. Lines, dots and slopes: whatever range
shows the variation, labelled honestly.

<Figure
  slug="viz-bar-charts-reach-bar-chart-inline-cutout"
  alt="Standing blocks of different heights over labelled trays, a straight rule laid across their tops"
  caption="The common baseline is the whole point, which is why cutting it misleads."
/>

One more, specific to waterfalls. A waterfall is a bridge from one total to
another: each bar begins where the last ended, and its direction says whether
the term added or took away. Direction is half the encoding, which is what
makes this failure possible.

<Chart slug="waterfall-sign-direction" />

Most misleading charts are wrong about emphasis, and a careful reader can
recover the truth from them. This one is wrong about *arithmetic*: the closing
figure is printed on the chart and it is not the right figure.

An `abs()` somewhere in the pipeline will do it, and so will a costs column
that arrives positive because that is how the ledger stores it. The check
takes five seconds: add the terms up yourself and see whether you land where
the last bar says.

The remaining mistakes are quicker to state:

- **Sorting alphabetically when value order would be clearer.**
- **Using a 3-D bar chart.** Just don't.
- **Drawing 50 bars on one chart** when a horizontal layout, a
  filter, or a "top 10" would be more useful.

## Check your understanding

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When is a **bar chart** the right choice?

- For comparing two quantitative variables.
  > Comparing two quantitative variables, to see if they are related, calls for a scatter plot, which places both variables on position axes. A bar chart requires one categorical and one quantitative axis.
- [o] For comparing a quantitative value across a small-to-medium number of discrete categories.
  > Bar charts use length-on-a-common-scale to encode quantity, which is one of the most accurate visual encodings. They're ideal when "how do these categories compare?" is the question.
- For showing the distribution of a single variable.
  > Distributions of a single variable are best shown with a histogram, which groups values into bins and reveals shape, spread, and skew. A bar chart requires a discrete categorical axis, not a continuous numeric range.
- For showing a continuous trend over time.
  > Continuous trends over time call for a line chart, which connects ordered time points with a continuous line. A bar chart treats the x-axis as discrete categories, losing the sense of continuous flow.

For time use a line chart; for distributions use a histogram; for two-variable relationships use a scatter plot.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why is **sorting bars by value** usually preferable to alphabetical order?

- It makes the chart render faster.
  > Rendering performance is not affected by whether bars are sorted. Sorting is a readability improvement, not a performance optimization.
- [o] The eye reads a ranked bar chart almost instantly, while alphabetical order forces the reader to do the ranking mentally.
  > The whole point of a bar chart is comparison. Sorting does the comparison work for the reader. Alphabetical order is essentially random in terms of the values, so it adds work without adding clarity.
- It is required by Plotly.
  > Plotly Express does not require sorting; it displays bars in whatever order the DataFrame rows are in. Sorting is a best-practice design decision, not a technical requirement.
- It uses less memory.
  > Sorting does not affect memory usage. The same data is stored regardless of its order in the DataFrame.

Two exceptions: categories with a natural order (months, sizes) and time-based categories should keep their natural order.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Truncating a bar chart's y-axis so it starts at, say, 90 instead of 0 is generally:

- [o] Misleading, bar length encodes magnitude *relative to zero*, so truncating exaggerates small differences and can mislead readers.
  > This is one of the classic ways charts lie. A bar starting at 90 and reaching 100 looks "very different" from a bar reaching 95, even though the real difference is 5%. The visual cue (length) no longer matches the data magnitude. For trend visualization where small differences matter, a line chart with a truncated axis is sometimes acceptable, for bar charts, almost never.
- Required for log-scale data.
  > Log scales change the spacing between values, but they do not require a non-zero baseline. Truncating a bar chart's y-axis is a separate design choice and is not needed for log-scale axes.
- A great way to save vertical space.
  > Saving space by cutting the y-axis is a poor trade-off: it breaks the visual contract that bar length represents the full magnitude from zero, causing readers to overestimate differences.
- The default behavior in Plotly Express.
  > Plotly Express sets the y-axis to start at zero by default for bar charts. Truncating the axis is something a developer would have to configure explicitly, so this is the opposite of the default.

If you want to emphasize a small difference, use a line chart or a dot plot, not a truncated bar chart.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`When should you use **horizontal** bars (**orientation="h"**) instead of vertical?

- Whenever the data is sorted.
  > Sorting bars by value is a best practice for both vertical and horizontal orientations. Sorting alone is not a reason to switch to horizontal, the choice is about label readability, not sort order.
- [o] When category labels are long, or when you have many categories that would otherwise crowd the x-axis.
  > Horizontal bars let the labels sit comfortably to the left of each bar, no rotation needed. They also let you stack a lot of categories vertically without crowding.
- When you have more than 100 categories.
  > Even with many categories, 100 bars are hard to read regardless of orientation. Beyond a practical limit, filtering to a top-N is better advice. Orientation choice is driven by label length, not category count alone.
- When you have fewer than 5 categories.
  > The number of categories is not a criterion for orientation, what matters is label length and readability. Short labels work fine vertically even with many categories; it is long labels that make horizontal orientation necessary.

A horizontal layout is often the small change that turns a cluttered chart into a readable one.`}
