We've spent a chapter on color, the heaviest extra channel. Two
others deserve their own short page: **size** and **shape** (or
*symbol*). Each adds an additional variable to a chart, but each
has narrow rules for when it's worth using.

## Size: a continuous third (or fourth) variable

Scale it on area, not radius, and the channel is *correct*. It is still a
narrow channel: both ends of the range fail, for different reasons.

<Chart slug="size-dynamic-range" />

We met `size=` in the chapter on bubble charts. The summary:

- Use `size` for a **continuous** variable.
- Use it when **rough magnitude** is informative, not when
  *precise* comparison matters.
- Combine with `color` and `size_max` to keep huge values from
  swallowing the chart.

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  datasets={[{ path: "palmer-penguins.csv", stageAs: "penguins.csv" }]}
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import pandas as pd
import plotly.express as px

# Palmer Penguins, the friendly stand-in for the iris dataset.
penguins = pd.read_csv("penguins.csv").dropna(
    subset=["bill_depth_mm", "bill_length_mm", "flipper_length_mm"]
)`,
      starterCode: `fig = px.scatter(
    penguins,
    x="bill_depth_mm",
    y="bill_length_mm",
    color="species",
    size="flipper_length_mm",  # the extra variable
    size_max=22,
    template="simple_white",
    title="Penguins: size encodes flipper length",
    labels={"bill_depth_mm": "Bill depth (mm)", "bill_length_mm": "Bill length (mm)"},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

The reader instantly sees that *Gentoo* penguins have the largest
flippers, without needing to read any individual value.

### When `size` goes wrong

- **Negative values** can't be areas. Filter or transform first.
- **A tiny range** of values produces visually indistinguishable
  bubbles. Use color instead.
- **Huge range** (millions to billions) makes a few bubbles
  enormous and the rest invisible. Take `np.log()` first, or
  filter the extremes.

## Shape (symbol): a categorical third variable

Shape has almost no capacity. Three or four values read at a glance, and
after that the reader is doing a lookup per point:

<Chart slug="shape-channel-capacity" />

`symbol="..."` gives each value of a categorical column its own
marker shape: circle, square, triangle, diamond, cross, etc.

<Figure
  slug="viz-size-and-shape-encodings-size-continuous-third-inline-cutout"
  alt="A row of chips growing steadily in size across a board while their positions stay fixed"
  caption="Size carries a third measure without moving anything on the chart."
/>

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  datasets={[{ path: "palmer-penguins.csv", stageAs: "penguins.csv" }]}
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import pandas as pd
import plotly.express as px

penguins = pd.read_csv("penguins.csv")`,
      starterCode: `fig = px.scatter(
    penguins,
    x="bill_depth_mm",
    y="bill_length_mm",
    color="species",
    symbol="species",
    template="simple_white",
    title="Penguins: redundant color and shape (helpful for B&W or colorblind)",
    labels={"bill_depth_mm": "Bill depth (mm)", "bill_length_mm": "Bill length (mm)"},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

We've encoded `species` *twice*: as both color and shape. That
sounds wasteful, but it's a legitimate **accessibility move**: the
chart still works in grayscale and for colorblind viewers.

### When shape is the better choice than color

- **Print-only context.** Colors may not print well; shapes always
  do.
- **Colorblind accessibility** as the *primary* encoding.
- **Very small markers**, where color hue is hard to perceive.

Often the answer is not to choose. Encode the group *twice*, and the chart
survives losing either one:

<Chart slug="redundant-encoding-helps" />

In most engineering redundancy is waste to be removed. In a chart it is what
makes the chart robust, for the same reason a spare tyre is: the second copy
is worthless until the first one fails, and then it is the only thing that
matters.

Color fails for boring reasons and it fails often: a laser printer, a
projector with the contrast wrong, a document converted to greyscale, or one
of roughly one man in twelve who cannot separate red from green. None of those
is an edge case, and none is visible from where you sit while making the
chart. The costs of carrying a second encoding are small and worth
knowing. Shape is a weak channel, so it handles four or five groups and not
twelve, telling shapes apart is a search rather than a glance, and the marks
have to be big enough to have a shape at all, which rules it out for a dense
scatter of thousands of points.

### When shape fails

Shape is a *weak* perceptual channel. Past about **6 categories**,
the eye can no longer reliably distinguish ●  ■  ▲  ▼  ◆  ★  ✦
✚ in a crowded plot. Stick to a handful.

The chart type that leans hardest on shape is the radar chart, and it has a
problem that has nothing to do with perception:

<Chart slug="radar-axis-order" />

Readers compare the enclosed polygons, and the enclosed area depends on which
spoke each variable landed on, which is usually a dataframe's column order.

The geometry is simple enough to check. The polygon is a ring of triangles,
one per neighbouring pair, and a triangle between adjacent spokes has area
proportional to the *product* of its two radii, so putting the two highest
scores side by side multiplies two large numbers while separating them pairs
each large one with a small one. Two more things to know before reaching for
one: the axes have to share a scale or the shape means nothing, which forces a
normalisation that also moves the shape, and the polygon closes, so the last
variable is adjacent to the first and one arbitrary pair gets extra weight
nobody notices.

## Combining size, color, and shape: just because you can…

Every channel below is mapped correctly. That is not enough:

<Chart slug="channel-overload" />

Plotly Express makes it easy to map four columns onto color, size,
shape, *and* faceting. But the more channels you use, the more the
reader has to *decode*. A 6-channel chart is a puzzle, not a
visualization.

<Figure
  slug="viz-size-and-shape-encodings-inline-cutout"
  alt="One row of identical chips growing in size, and another row keeping one size but changing shape"
  caption="Size suits a quantity and shape suits a category, and each channel costs effort."
/>

A working rule:

- **1 variable** → x or y; you're done.
- **2 variables** → x and y; you're done.
- **3 variables** → add color.
- **4 variables** → add size (if continuous) or facet.
- **5+ variables** → start asking whether the *chart* is the right
  format or whether you need a *dashboard*.

## A worked example

Try this. Read out loud what each `=` is encoding:

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      starterCode: `import plotly.express as px

df = px.data.gapminder().query("year == 2007")

fig = px.scatter(
    df,
    x="gdpPercap",
    y="lifeExp",
    color="continent",
    size="pop",
    symbol="continent",  # redundant with color
    log_x=True,
    size_max=55,
    template="simple_white",
    title="Gapminder 2007, 4 encodings (color & symbol redundant)",
    labels={"gdpPercap": "GDP per capita (USD, log)", "lifeExp": "Life expectancy"},
)
fig.show()
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

You should say: "x = GDP, y = life expectancy, color = continent
(also shape), size = population." Reading the call aloud is the
best way to make sure the chart matches your intention.
