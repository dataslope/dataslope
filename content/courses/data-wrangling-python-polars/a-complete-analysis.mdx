**The question:** in the diamonds dataset, the *best* cut grade has
the *lowest* average price. Is that real, and if so, why?

## Step 3: form a hypothesis and test it

Diamond price is dominated by **carat**, not by cut. If ideal-cut
stones are systematically smaller, that alone would explain it.

<Figure
  slug="pl-a-complete-analysis-step-3-form-hypothesis-inline-cutout"
  alt="A written guess pinned above a bench where two groups of chips are weighed against each other, the pointer settling off the mark"
  caption="State the guess first, then let the comparison decide it rather than the other way round."
/>

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  datasets={[{ path: "diamonds.csv", stageAs: "diamonds.csv" }]}
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import polars as pl
GRADES = pl.Enum(["Fair", "Good", "Very Good", "Premium", "Ideal"])
diamonds = pl.read_csv("diamonds.csv").with_columns(
    pl.col("cut").cast(GRADES)
)`,
      starterCode: `display(
    diamonds.group_by("cut")
    .agg(
        pl.len().alias("n"),
        pl.col("carat").mean().round(3).alias("avg_carat"),
        pl.col("price").mean().round(0).alias("avg_price"),
        (pl.col("price") / pl.col("carat")).mean().round(0).alias("avg_per_carat"),
    )
    .sort("cut")
)
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

Half the answer is there. Ideal diamonds average 0.70 carat against
Fair's 1.05, so the best-graded stones really are the smallest.

Per-carat price is only half convincing, though. It climbs from
3,767 for Fair to 4,223 for Premium, and then *drops* to 3,920 for
Ideal. Dividing by carat assumes price is proportional to weight, and
it is not: a two-carat stone costs far more than twice a one-carat
one. So the ratio over-corrects for the largest stones and
under-corrects for the smallest. It is a hint, not a control.

## Step 4: control for the confounder properly

The honest version compares diamonds of similar size: bucket by
carat, then look at cut within each bucket.

<Figure
  slug="pl-a-complete-analysis-step-4-control-confounder-inline-cutout"
  alt="Chips sorted first into bands of matching size and only then compared within each band, the earlier mixed comparison pushed aside"
  caption="Compare within bands of equal carat and the reversal in the raw averages disappears."
/>

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  datasets={[{ path: "diamonds.csv", stageAs: "diamonds.csv" }]}
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import polars as pl
GRADES = pl.Enum(["Fair", "Good", "Very Good", "Premium", "Ideal"])
diamonds = pl.read_csv("diamonds.csv").with_columns(
    pl.col("cut").cast(GRADES)
)`,
      starterCode: `banded = (
    diamonds.lazy()
    .with_columns(
        band=pl.when(pl.col("carat") < 0.5)
        .then(pl.lit("under 0.5ct"))
        .when(pl.col("carat") < 1.0)
        .then(pl.lit("0.5 to 1ct"))
        .when(pl.col("carat") < 1.5)
        .then(pl.lit("1 to 1.5ct"))
        .otherwise(pl.lit("1.5ct and up"))
    )
    .group_by("band", "cut")
    .agg(
        pl.len().alias("n"),
        pl.col("price").median().alias("median_price"),
    )
    .collect()
)

BANDS = ["under 0.5ct", "0.5 to 1ct", "1 to 1.5ct", "1.5ct and up"]

display(
    banded.pivot(on="cut", index="band", values="median_price")
    # pivot names columns in the order it met them, so restate the order
    .select("band", "Fair", "Good", "Very Good", "Premium", "Ideal")
    .sort(pl.col("band").cast(pl.Enum(BANDS)))
)
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

Read across each row, worst cut to best. In the two bands that carry
the money, the reversal is gone and the expected ordering is clear:
at 1 to 1.5 carats a Fair stone's median is 4,209 and an Ideal one's
is 6,323. The `under 0.5ct` row is the ragged one, which is what a
band holding the cheapest stones tends to look like.

So the original finding was Simpson's paradox: an aggregate that
reverses direction once you condition on the confounding variable.

## Step 5: quantify it

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  datasets={[{ path: "diamonds.csv", stageAs: "diamonds.csv" }]}
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import polars as pl
diamonds = pl.read_csv("diamonds.csv")`,
      starterCode: `display(
    diamonds.select(
        carat_price=pl.corr("carat", "price").round(3),
        table_price=pl.corr("table", "price").round(3),
        depth_price=pl.corr("depth", "price").round(3),
    )
)

# The premium a better cut earns, holding size roughly constant
display(
    diamonds.filter(pl.col("carat").is_between(0.9, 1.1))
    .group_by("cut")
    .agg(
        pl.len().alias("n"),
        pl.col("price").median().alias("median_price"),
    )
    .sort("median_price")
)
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

Carat correlates with price at 0.922; `table` manages 0.127 and
`depth` is essentially zero. Among near-one-carat stones the cut
ordering behaves: Fair 3,730 rising to Ideal 5,220, about a 1,500
dollar spread. Set that against the carat effect, where the median
jumps from roughly 2,300 in the 0.5-to-1 band to 12,700 above 1.5
carats, and the relative sizes are clear.

## Step 6: write it as one pipeline

The exploratory version was many cells. The version you keep is one
lazy pipeline that another person can read.

<Figure
  slug="pl-a-complete-analysis-step-6-write-as-inline-cutout"
  alt="Eight small stations bolted onto one continuous rail, a single load entering at one end and leaving finished at the other"
  caption="Eight ideas from the course arrive as one expression, evaluated in a single pass."
/>

<CodeBlock
  adapter="python"
  datasets={[{ path: "diamonds.csv", stageAs: "diamonds.csv" }]}
  files={[
    {
      filename: "main.py",
      initCode: `import polars as pl`,
      starterCode: `GRADES = pl.Enum(["Fair", "Good", "Very Good", "Premium", "Ideal"])

def carat_band() -> pl.Expr:
    """Size bands used throughout this analysis."""
    carat = pl.col("carat")
    return (
        pl.when(carat < 0.5).then(pl.lit("under 0.5ct"))
        .when(carat < 1.0).then(pl.lit("0.5 to 1ct"))
        .when(carat < 1.5).then(pl.lit("1 to 1.5ct"))
        .otherwise(pl.lit("1.5ct and up"))
        .alias("band")
    )

report = (
    pl.scan_csv("diamonds.csv")
    .with_columns(pl.col("cut").cast(GRADES), carat_band())
    .group_by("band", "cut")
    .agg(
        pl.len().alias("n"),
        pl.col("carat").mean().round(2).alias("avg_carat"),
        pl.col("price").median().alias("median_price"),
        (pl.col("price") / pl.col("carat")).median().round(0).alias("per_carat"),
    )
    .sort("band", "cut")
    .collect()
)

display(report)
display(report.select(pl.col("n").sum()))
`,
    },
  ]}
/>

Eight ideas from this course appear in that block: `scan_csv()`, an
`Enum`, an expression factory, `when/then`, multi-key `group_by()`,
several aggregations, a sort, and one `collect()` at the end.

## The finding

> Ideal-cut diamonds have the lowest average price not because a
> better cut is worth less, but because ideal-cut stones in this
> dataset are systematically smaller. Carat drives price (r = 0.922),
> and average carat falls from 1.05 for Fair to 0.70 for Ideal.
> Within a size band the expected ordering returns: among stones of
> 1 to 1.5 carats, the median rises from 4,209 for Fair to 6,323 for
> Ideal. Cut is worth roughly 1,500 dollars on a one-carat stone,
> against a carat effect measured in five figures.

That is a defensible answer: it states the effect, the mechanism, the
evidence, and the size of the real effect once the confounder is
controlled for.

present and stronger than it was for cut (D averages 0.66 carat, J

from a case where the shortcut visibly fails.

A diagnostic that gives you the answer you expected on one variable
and refuses on the next is a diagnostic worth keeping.

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What made the original "better cut, lower price" finding misleading?

- The mean was distorted by a few extremely expensive outliers
  > Outliers do pull the mean, but the reversal survives when medians are used, so that is not the cause here.
- The cut grades were compared in alphabetical rather than quality order
  > Ordering the grades correctly is what made the pattern legible; it did not create the pattern.
- [o] Cut is confounded with carat, and carat is what actually drives price
  > Conditioning on the confounder makes the reversal largely disappear, which is Simpson's paradox.
- The dataset contains duplicate rows that over-count ideal-cut stones
  > Duplicates would inflate counts, not reverse a price ordering, and the counts here are legitimate.

An aggregate that reverses when you condition on a third variable is the signature to watch for.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why bucket by carat rather than just dividing price by carat?

- Division by carat fails when carat is zero
  > No diamond in the dataset has a carat of zero, so the division is well defined throughout.
- Per-carat price cannot be computed inside a \`group_by()\`
  > It can, and the course does exactly that; the objection is to what the number assumes.
- [o] Price is not proportional to carat, so a per-carat ratio still favours one size range
  > Comparing like with like inside a band makes fewer assumptions than dividing does.
- Bucketing is faster than computing a ratio on every row
  > Bucketing adds work rather than saving it; the reason to prefer it is honesty, not speed.

Price rises faster than linearly with carat, which is why the ratio is a crude control rather than a clean one.`}
