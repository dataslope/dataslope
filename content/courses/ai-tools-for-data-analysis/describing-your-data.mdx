Most bad answers are bad because of a bad question, and most bad
questions are bad because they omitted the data. The model is not
guessing out of carelessness. It is guessing because guessing is the
only option you left it.

## The four-part description

Before asking for anything, supply these four things. Together they
take about thirty seconds to assemble and they remove the majority of
schema-related errors.

<Steps>

<Step>
### The schema

Column names and types. The literal output of `df.dtypes`, or a
`CREATE TABLE` statement, or even a hand-typed list. Names matter most,
because inventing names is the model's single most common failure.
</Step>

<Step>
### The grain

**What does one row represent?** One order? One order line? One
customer-month? This one sentence prevents more aggregation errors
than any other input, because almost every double-counting bug traces
back to a misunderstood grain.
</Step>

<Step>
### The quirks

The things only you know. Test accounts are still in the table. `status`
was backfilled in March 2024 and is unreliable before then. `amount` is
in cents. Refunds appear as negative rows. Nulls in `region` mean
"unknown", not "none".
</Step>

<Step>
### The question, with its success criterion

Not "analyze this" but "I want one row per region with total revenue
for calendar year 2025, excluding refunds and test accounts."
</Step>

</Steps>

## A worked comparison

Here is a weak prompt:

```text
I have some sales data. What were our best regions last year?
```

And a strong one:

```text
Table: sales_lines
One row per line item on an order (an order with 3 products
is 3 rows).

Columns:
  order_id      INTEGER
  line_id       INTEGER
  customer_id   INTEGER
  region        TEXT     -- NULL means unknown, ~4% of rows
  order_date    DATE
  quantity      INTEGER
  unit_price_cents INTEGER  -- cents, not dollars
  is_refund     BOOLEAN

Quirks: refunds appear as separate rows with is_refund = true
and positive quantity. Test accounts have customer_id < 1000.

Question: total net revenue per region for calendar 2025,
excluding refunds and test accounts, sorted descending.
Net revenue = quantity * unit_price_cents / 100.
```

The second one takes a minute to write and the answer is far more
likely to be usable. More importantly, the second one is *checkable*:
you have written down what correct means, so you can tell whether the
answer meets it.

<Callout type="info" title="Why writing the prompt is itself useful">
Analysts routinely discover, while writing the strong version, that
they did not actually know the grain, or had forgotten about refunds.
The prompt is a specification, and writing a specification finds holes
in your own understanding. That benefit accrues even if you never send
the message.
</Callout>

## What not to bother with

A few things people add that do not help much:

* **"You are an expert data analyst."** Role preambles have a small
  effect at best on current models. The schema is worth a hundred of
  them.
* **"Think step by step."** Current models already reason before
  answering when the task calls for it. Explicit instructions to think
  are largely redundant.
* **Politeness and threats alike.** Neither "please" nor "this is very
  important to my career" reliably changes correctness.

Spend the effort on the schema, the grain, and the quirks instead.

## Concept check

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Which single sentence prevents the most aggregation errors?

- A note about how many rows the table contains.
  > Row count tells the model about scale, not about structure.
- [o] A statement of what one row represents.
  > Grain determines whether summing a column double-counts, and most double-counting bugs trace directly to a misunderstood grain.
- A description of your preferred output formatting.
  > Formatting has no bearing on correctness of the computation.
- A statement of which SQL dialect you are using.
  > Dialect affects syntax rather than whether the aggregation logic is right.

Grain is the fact that determines whether an aggregate means anything.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Your \`sales_lines\` table has one row per line item. You ask for "total revenue per order" and the model writes \`SUM(unit_price_cents)\` grouped by \`order_id\`. What is wrong?

- \`SUM\` cannot be used with \`GROUP BY order_id\`.
  > That combination is standard and correct.
- The result should be divided by the number of line items.
  > Averaging is not what "total revenue" means.
- Grouping by \`order_id\` produces one row per customer.
  > It produces one row per order, which is what was asked for.
- [o] It ignores \`quantity\`, so it sums unit prices rather than line totals.
  > Revenue for a line is quantity times unit price; summing the unit price alone undercounts every line with quantity above one.

The model used a plausible-looking column and skipped the one that
makes the arithmetic correct.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Which of these belongs in the "quirks" part of a data description?

- [o] That \`amount\` is stored in cents rather than dollars.
  > This is invisible from the schema, silently produces answers off by a factor of 100, and is exactly the kind of fact only you know.
- The name of the database engine hosting the table.
  > Engine affects syntax, not the meaning of the data.
- That you would like the results sorted descending.
  > Sort order is part of the request, not a property of the data.
- The number of columns in the table.
  > Column count is visible from the schema itself.

Quirks are the facts that are true of your data and recoverable from
nowhere else.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Why does writing a detailed prompt have value even before you send it?

- Because the assistant stores your description for future sessions.
  > Ordinary chat sessions do not persist a schema for later reuse.
- Because longer prompts are billed at a lower rate per token.
  > Longer prompts cost more, not less.
- Because models refuse to answer prompts under a certain length.
  > There is no minimum length requirement.
- [o] Because specifying the task precisely exposes gaps in your own understanding.
  > Analysts regularly discover while writing that they did not know the grain, or had forgotten refunds existed.

A specification is a thinking tool. Writing one finds the questions you
had not asked yourself.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`You write "NULL in \`region\` means unknown, about 4% of rows". Which downstream error does this most directly prevent?

- The model using an inner join instead of a left join.
  > Join type is a separate decision about table relationships.
- The model choosing the wrong SQL dialect.
  > Null semantics carry no information about dialect.
- [o] The model silently dropping those rows and reporting a total that is 4% short.
  > Knowing nulls are meaningful prompts an explicit decision about them instead of an unexamined filter.
- The model sorting the output incorrectly.
  > Sort direction is unaffected by null semantics.

Telling the model that missing data is *meaningful* changes whether it
gets quietly discarded.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`Which of these prompt additions has the *least* effect on answer correctness?

- [o] Opening with "You are a world-class data scientist."
  > Role preambles have small and inconsistent effects on current models, well below what supplying the schema achieves.
- Stating that refunds appear as negative amounts.
  > This directly changes whether totals are computed correctly.
- Defining what counts as an active customer.
  > Definitional clarity is often the difference between a right and a wrong answer.
- Pasting the output of \`df.dtypes\`.
  > Types prevent a range of real errors, particularly around dates and numeric strings.

Persona framing is cheap and mostly decorative. Schema and definitions
are where the leverage is.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`What makes a prompt *checkable*?

- Requesting that the answer be formatted as a table.
  > Presentation does not define correctness.
- Keeping it under 200 words so it is easy to reread.
  > Brevity aids reading but does not establish what correct means.
- [o] Stating in advance what a correct answer must contain.
  > Writing down the expected shape, filters, and definition gives you a standard to compare the output against.
- Asking the model to rate its own confidence at the end.
  > Self-reported confidence is poorly calibrated and adds no external standard.

If you cannot say what correct looks like before you see the answer,
you cannot evaluate the answer you get.`}
/>

<MultipleChoice
  markdown={`An analyst supplies a perfect schema but omits the grain. Which answer is most likely to come back wrong?

- "Rename these columns to snake_case."
  > A purely syntactic transformation that grain does not affect.
- [o] "What is our average order value?"
  > At line-item grain the model may average line amounts rather than order totals, producing a number that is plausible and wrong.
- "What data type is \`order_date\`?"
  > Types are given directly in the schema.
- "How many distinct columns are in this table?"
  > This is answerable from the schema alone.

Grain matters exactly when the question aggregates across rows, which
