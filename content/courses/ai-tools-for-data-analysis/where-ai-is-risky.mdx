---
title: Where AI Is Genuinely Risky
description: The tasks where the verification cost equals or exceeds the production cost, which is where delegation stops paying.
---

Flip the rule from the last chapter. When checking an answer costs as
much as producing it, or when there is no way to check it at all, the
economics of delegation collapse.

## Statistical inference

Asking for a t-test gets you a t-test. What you needed was for
somebody to consider whether a t-test was appropriate: whether the
observations are independent, whether the variances are comparable,
whether the sample is large enough for the distributional assumption
to hold, and whether you have run twelve other comparisons on the same
data without correcting for it.

The model will name a test and report a p-value. Both will be
correctly computed and possibly meaningless. Verifying the choice of
test requires the statistical understanding that would have let you
choose it yourself, so the delegation saved nothing and added a layer
of false authority.

## Causal claims

Covered in *Asking for Interpretation* and worth restating: no amount
of data supports a causal claim without a design that identifies it.
An assistant given a correlation will supply a causal story on
request, and often without being asked.

## Anything requiring institutional knowledge

* Whether the `is_active` flag was backfilled correctly.
* Whether the 2024 spike is real or an artifact of the tracking
  migration.
* Whether "customer" in this table means account or billing entity.
* Whether the finance team's revenue definition includes deferred
  revenue.

These are facts about your organization. They exist in people's heads
and in internal documents, not in the training corpus. The model will
answer anyway, with a plausible general-case answer.

<Callout type="warn" title="The tell">
The riskiest answers are the ones that are *generically correct*.
"Revenue should exclude refunds" is true in general and may be wrong
for your reporting standard. Generic correctness is exactly what a
model is good at producing, and exactly what does not transfer to a
specific organization.
</Callout>

## Metric definitions

Related but distinct. There is often no fact of the matter about
whether "active user" means logged in this week or performed a core
action this month. There is only a convention your organization
adopted. A model asked to define it will produce a defensible
definition, which will silently differ from the one your dashboards
use, and the two numbers will then disagree forever.

## High-stakes, low-verifiability outputs

The combination is what matters. A board slide asserting a cause is
high-stakes and unverifiable. A regulatory filing is high-stakes and
consequential. A model-written summary that will be quoted by people
who never see the underlying data is the worst case, because the
verification opportunity is lost permanently at the moment it is
circulated.

## The decision rule

Ask two questions before delegating:

1. **Can I check this faster than I could produce it?**
2. **If it is wrong and I do not catch it, what happens?**

Delegate freely when the answers are "yes" and "little". Delegate with
a checklist when they are "yes" and "a lot". Do it yourself when the
answer to the first is "no".
